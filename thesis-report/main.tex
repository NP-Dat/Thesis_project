\begin{center}
  \textbf{ABSTRACT}
\end{center}

\vspace{1cm}

Occupational burnout is a growing concern in industrial workplaces, where
high workloads, repetitive tasks, and shift-based schedules place sustained
psychological pressure on employees. Traditional approaches to burnout
detection rely on periodic surveys and manager observation, which are
reactive rather than preventive. By the time symptoms become visible,
productivity losses and health consequences have often already occurred.

This thesis presents the design and implementation of an intelligent
web-based framework for the early detection of employee burnout in
industrial settings. The system combines a validated psychometric
instrument---the \textbf{Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI)}---with a
\textbf{Random Forest} regression model to predict a continuous burn rate
score from three features: employee designation, resource allocation, and a
mental fatigue score derived from the CBI quiz responses.

The framework follows a microservice architecture comprising three
components. A \textbf{React/Next.js} frontend provides two role-based
portals: an employee portal for self-assessment, trend tracking, and access
to support resources, and an admin portal with department-level heatmaps,
risk distribution charts, KPI analytics, and an automated alert inbox. A
\textbf{Node.js/Express} backend manages authentication, quiz scoring, risk
classification, alert generation, and dashboard aggregation, backed by
\textbf{MySQL} for relational data and \textbf{MongoDB} for the quiz bank
and raw submissions. A \textbf{Python/FastAPI} microservice loads the
trained model at startup and serves predictions via a RESTful endpoint,
decoupling the machine learning concern from the application backend.

The Random Forest model, trained on approximately 22{,}000 records from a
public Kaggle dataset, achieves a coefficient of determination
($R^{2}$) of approximately 0.90, a mean absolute error (MAE) of
approximately 0.048, and a root mean squared error (RMSE) of approximately
0.061 on the held-out test set, outperforming the Linear Regression baseline
across all three metrics. Feature importance analysis confirms that the
mental fatigue score is the dominant predictor, accounting for over 80\% of
the model's predictive power.

Several limitations remain. The HR profile data used for designation and
resource allocation is simulated rather than sourced from a real factory
system. The model uses only three features, and the evaluation relies on a
single public dataset that may not generalize across all industrial
domains. No formal usability study was conducted. Despite these
constraints, the system demonstrates a viable architecture for integrating
psychometric assessment, machine learning prediction, and organizational
analytics into a unified platform for proactive burnout management.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} employee burnout detection, Copenhagen Burnout
Inventory, Random Forest regression, microservices architecture, industrial
workforce, web-based health monitoring.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}

The industrial workforce operates under conditions that are uniquely
conducive to occupational burnout. Factory employees routinely face
physically demanding tasks, rigid shift schedules, production quotas, and
limited autonomy over their working conditions. The World Health
Organization recognised burnout as an occupational phenomenon in the
eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11),
defining it as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has
not been successfully managed~\cite{who2019}. The three defining dimensions
of burnout---emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, and reduced personal
accomplishment---manifest in measurable declines in productivity, increased
absenteeism, higher turnover rates, and elevated healthcare
costs~\cite{maslach2001}.

Global estimates suggest that stress-related conditions, including burnout,
cost businesses hundreds of billions of dollars annually through lost
productivity and medical expenses~\cite{gallup2024}. In manufacturing and
industrial sectors, the consequences extend beyond individual well-being:
fatigued workers are more prone to safety incidents, quality defects, and
equipment damage. Despite these risks, most organisations still rely on
ad-hoc observation and infrequent surveys to gauge employee mental health,
an approach that is inherently reactive rather than preventive.

Advances in machine learning (ML) and web technologies have created an
opportunity to shift burnout management from reactive response to proactive
detection. By combining validated psychometric instruments with predictive
models and real-time dashboards, it is now feasible to build systems that
continuously assess burnout risk at both the individual and organisational
level. Such systems can alert management to emerging problems before they
escalate, while simultaneously empowering employees with self-monitoring
tools and timely access to support resources.

\subsection{Motivating Scenario and Use Cases}

Consider a mid-sized manufacturing plant employing 200 workers distributed
across six departments, operating on rotating day and night shifts. The
plant's HR team consists of three staff members who are responsible for
monitoring attendance, handling grievances, and managing benefits. Under
current practice, identifying a worker at risk of burnout depends on a
supervisor noticing visible signs of distress---chronic fatigue, irritability,
or declining output---and escalating the concern to HR. By the time this
occurs, the worker may have already experienced weeks or months of
deteriorating mental health, and the department may have absorbed
measurable productivity losses.

This thesis envisions a system that transforms this reactive workflow into a
proactive one. Two primary use cases motivate the design:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Employee self-assessment and trend tracking.} Each worker
    periodically completes a short 13-question burnout quiz on a
    web-based portal. The system immediately calculates a predicted burn
    rate using a machine learning model and presents the result through
    an intuitive gauge visualisation. Historical trends are displayed as
    line charts, enabling the worker to observe how their burnout risk
    evolves over time. If the predicted risk is high or critical, the
    system surfaces curated mental health resources directly on the
    results page.

  \item \textbf{HR department-level monitoring and alerts.} The
    administrative portal aggregates assessment data across the entire
    workforce. A department heatmap visualises average burn rates by
    organisational unit, allowing HR to identify which departments are
    under the greatest strain at a glance. Automated alerts notify HR
    when a worker's burn rate crosses a critical threshold or spikes
    sharply between consecutive assessments. Drill-down views expose
    anonymised employee-level detail, preserving privacy while enabling
    targeted intervention.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Despite the recognised impact of burnout on industrial productivity and
worker welfare, several gaps persist in current practice:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Detection is reactive.} Most organisations identify
    burnout only after its effects---absenteeism, errors, resignations---have
    already materialised. There is no systematic mechanism for early
    warning.

  \item \textbf{Psychometric data is underutilised.} Validated burnout
    instruments such as the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI) exist and
    are freely available, yet they are rarely integrated into continuous
    monitoring workflows. When surveys are conducted, the results are
    typically analysed manually and in isolation.

  \item \textbf{No predictive capability.} Traditional approaches report
    current symptoms but do not predict future risk. Machine learning
    can bridge this gap by learning patterns from historical data and
    estimating burn rates from a small number of measurable inputs.

  \item \textbf{No organisational-level visibility.} Individual survey
    results remain siloed. HR managers lack aggregated, visual tools
    such as department heatmaps, risk distribution charts, and
    trend analyses that would enable data-driven workforce
    management decisions.
\end{enumerate}

This thesis addresses these gaps by designing and implementing an
integrated web-based framework that combines psychometric assessment,
machine learning prediction, and multi-level analytics within a single
platform.

\subsection{Scope and Objectives}

The scope of this thesis is the design, implementation, and evaluation of a
desktop web application for early burnout detection in industrial
workplaces. The system uses simulated HR data (since real factory records
are private and inaccessible), the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory as the
psychometric instrument, and a Random Forest regression model trained on a
public dataset as the predictive engine.

The specific objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Implement a web-based Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI)
    assessment that converts employee responses into a mental fatigue
    score suitable for machine learning input.

  \item Train and evaluate a Random Forest regression model to predict
    employee burn rate from three features: designation, resource
    allocation, and mental fatigue score.

  \item Build an employee self-monitoring portal with burn rate gauge,
    historical trend charts, section score breakdowns, and contextual
    access to mental health resources.

  \item Build an administrative command centre with department heatmaps,
    risk distribution analytics, KPI correlation views, and a drill-down
    capability for individual employee analysis.

  \item Implement an automated alert system that notifies administrators
    when employees cross critical risk thresholds or exhibit sharp
    burn rate increases between assessments.

  \item Demonstrate a microservice architecture that cleanly separates
    the machine learning inference concern (Python/FastAPI) from the
    application backend (Node.js/Express) and the user interface
    (React/Next.js).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research Questions}

This thesis investigates the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1.}] How effectively can a Random Forest regression
    model predict employee burn rate using only three input features
    (designation, resource allocation, and mental fatigue score)?

  \item[\textbf{RQ2.}] Can a web-based system provide actionable burnout
    insights to both employees (through self-monitoring tools) and HR
    administrators (through department-level analytics and automated
    alerts)?

  \item[\textbf{RQ3.}] What are the practical benefits and trade-offs of
    using a microservice architecture to separate machine learning
    inference from the application backend in a health monitoring
    system?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Assumptions and Proposed Solution}

The following assumptions underlie the design of the system:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulated HR data.} Real factory HR databases are
    private and inaccessible for academic research. The backend
    generates synthetic HR profiles (designation, resource allocation,
    department, shift type) at registration time, drawing values from
    the same ranges as the training dataset to ensure the ML model
    receives in-distribution inputs.

  \item \textbf{CBI as a valid burnout measure.} The Copenhagen Burnout
    Inventory is an established, freely available instrument with
    demonstrated reliability and validity in occupational health
    research. Its 13-question excerpt (personal and work-related
    burnout sections) is used as the sole actively collected data
    point.

  \item \textbf{Desktop-only usage.} The system targets HR office
    terminals and factory kiosk stations. No mobile-responsive layout
    is implemented.

  \item \textbf{Single-language interface.} The user interface is
    presented in English. Multi-language support is deferred to future
    work.
\end{itemize}

The proposed solution is a three-tier web application:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{React/Next.js frontend} with two role-based portals
    (employee and admin), connected to the backend via RESTful API
    calls, with TanStack Query managing server state.

  \item A \textbf{Node.js/Express backend} that handles authentication
    (JWT), quiz scoring (CBI with reverse scoring), risk classification,
    alert generation, dashboard aggregation, and resource management,
    backed by MySQL and MongoDB.

  \item A \textbf{Python/FastAPI AI microservice} that loads a pre-trained
    Random Forest model at startup and exposes a single prediction
    endpoint. The backend can switch between the real AI service and an
    in-process mock formula via an environment variable, enabling
    independent development and deployment of each component.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Occupational Burnout}

\subsubsection{Definition and Impact on the Industrial Workforce}

The concept of occupational burnout was first introduced by Freudenberger
in 1974 to describe the state of physical and emotional depletion observed
among human service workers~\cite{freudenberger1974}. The construct was
later formalised by Maslach and Jackson, who defined burnout as a
three-dimensional syndrome comprising emotional exhaustion,
depersonalisation (cynicism), and reduced personal
accomplishment~\cite{maslach1981}. In 2019 the World Health Organization
included burnout in the ICD-11 as an occupational phenomenon, describing it
as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been
successfully managed~\cite{who2019}.

In industrial and manufacturing environments, the consequences of burnout
extend beyond individual suffering. Research has linked burnout to
increased absenteeism, higher employee turnover, reduced product quality,
and elevated workplace accident rates~\cite{ahola2017}. A Gallup
meta-analysis across industries found that employees experiencing burnout
are 63\% more likely to take a sick day, 2.6 times more likely to seek a
new job, and 13\% less confident in their performance~\cite{gallup2024}.
For factory settings with tight production schedules and safety-critical
processes, these figures translate directly into operational and financial
risk.

\subsubsection{Burnout Measurement Instruments}

The two most widely used instruments for measuring occupational burnout are
the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) and the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory
(CBI).

The \textbf{Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI)}, developed in
1981~\cite{maslach1981}, is the most cited burnout measure in the
literature. It consists of 22 items across three subscales (emotional
exhaustion, depersonalisation, and personal accomplishment). However, the
MBI is copyrighted and requires a commercial licence for use, which limits
its adoption in academic and open-source projects.

The \textbf{Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI)}, developed by Kristensen
et~al.\ in 2005~\cite{kristensen2005}, was created as a freely available
alternative. The CBI conceptualises burnout as a state of physical and
psychological fatigue and exhaustion, measured along three dimensions:
personal burnout, work-related burnout, and client-related burnout. Each
dimension is assessed through a set of questions scored on a five-point
frequency scale ranging from ``Always'' (100 points) to ``Never/almost
never'' (0 points). The CBI has demonstrated high internal consistency
(Cronbach's alpha exceeding 0.85 across all subscales) and strong
predictive validity for outcomes such as sickness absence and sleep
disturbance~\cite{kristensen2005}.

\subsubsection{The Copenhagen Burnout Inventory in This Thesis}

This thesis adopts the CBI for several reasons. First, the CBI is
completely free to use, with no licensing restrictions, making it ideal for
an academic project. Second, the CBI distinguishes between personal and
work-related burnout, which allows the system to provide more granular
feedback to both employees and administrators. Third, the CBI's
straightforward frequency-based response scale maps naturally to a numeric
score that can be fed into a machine learning model.

Since the target population consists of industrial workers rather than
client-facing professionals, the client-related burnout subscale is omitted.
The system uses the remaining 13 questions: six for personal burnout and
seven for work-related burnout. One question (``Do you have enough energy
for family and friends during leisure time?'') is reverse-scored, as a
positive response indicates lower burnout. The section averages are combined
and normalised to a 0--10 scale to produce the mental fatigue score used as
input to the machine learning model.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Health Prediction}

\subsubsection{Supervised Regression}

Machine learning tasks are broadly categorised into supervised and
unsupervised learning. In supervised learning, the algorithm is trained on
a labelled dataset where each input is associated with a known
output~\cite{bishop2006}. When the output is a continuous numeric value,
the task is called regression.

Burnout prediction fits naturally into the supervised regression paradigm:
the training dataset contains employee observations with a known burn rate
(the target variable), and the goal is to learn a function that maps
employee features to a predicted burn rate for new, unseen employees.

\subsubsection{Linear Regression}

Linear Regression is the simplest regression algorithm. It models the
relationship between the input features and the target as a linear
equation:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{y} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + w_3 x_3 + b
  \label{eq:linear-regression}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $x_1, x_2, x_3$ are the input features (designation,
resource allocation, mental fatigue score), $w_1, w_2, w_3$ are learned
weights, and $b$ is a bias term. The weights are optimised by minimising
the sum of squared errors between predicted and actual target
values~\cite{hastie2009}.

Linear Regression serves as a baseline model in this thesis because its
simplicity provides a performance floor against which more complex models
can be compared. Its learned weights are directly interpretable: a larger
weight on mental fatigue score, for example, indicates that fatigue is the
dominant predictor of burn rate.

\subsubsection{Random Forest Regression}

Random Forest is an ensemble learning method introduced by
Breiman~\cite{breiman2001}. It constructs a collection of decision trees,
each trained on a random bootstrap sample of the training data and using a
random subset of features at each split. The final prediction is the
average of all individual tree predictions.

Random Forest offers several advantages over Linear Regression for this
problem:

\begin{itemize}
  \item It captures non-linear relationships and interaction effects
    between features without requiring manual feature engineering.
  \item It is robust to outliers, since individual trees split on
    thresholds rather than fitting a global line.
  \item It is invariant to feature scaling, meaning that features with
    different ranges (e.g., designation 1--5 vs.\ resource allocation
    1--10) do not require normalisation.
  \item It provides built-in feature importance scores, which quantify
    each feature's contribution to predictive accuracy.
\end{itemize}

For tabular datasets with a small number of features and tens of thousands
of rows, Random Forest consistently delivers strong performance with
minimal hyperparameter tuning, making it a practical choice for a
thesis-scale project~\cite{fernandez2014}.

\subsubsection{Model Evaluation Metrics}

Three complementary metrics are used to evaluate regression model
performance:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE):} The average of the absolute
    differences between predicted and actual values. On a 0--1 burn rate
    scale, an MAE of 0.05 means the model is typically off by 5
    percentage points.

  \item \textbf{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):} The square root of the
    mean of squared errors. RMSE penalises large errors more heavily
    than MAE, making it sensitive to occasional outlier predictions.

  \item \textbf{Coefficient of Determination ($R^{2}$):} The proportion
    of variance in the target variable explained by the model. An
    $R^{2}$ of 0.90 means the model explains 90\% of the variation in
    burn rates. Values closer to 1.0 indicate better fit.
\end{itemize}

Reporting all three metrics together provides a comprehensive view: MAE
for average-case accuracy, RMSE for worst-case sensitivity, and $R^{2}$
for overall explanatory power~\cite{hastie2009}.

\subsection{Web Application Architectures}

\subsubsection{Microservices vs.\ Monolithic Architecture}

A monolithic architecture packages the entire application---user interface,
business logic, data access, and any ML inference---into a single
deployable unit. This approach is simple to develop initially but becomes
difficult to maintain and scale as the application grows, especially when
different concerns have different technology requirements~\cite{newman2015}.

A microservice architecture decomposes the application into small,
independently deployable services, each responsible for a single
concern~\cite{newman2015}. Services communicate via lightweight protocols
such as HTTP/REST. The key benefits include independent deployment, technology
heterogeneity (e.g., using Python for ML and JavaScript for web
logic), fault isolation, and team scalability.

This thesis adopts a microservice approach because the machine learning
inference component has fundamentally different requirements from the web
application: it requires Python and scikit-learn, while the application
backend benefits from the JavaScript ecosystem (Express, Mongoose). By
separating the AI service into its own FastAPI microservice, the system
achieves clean separation of concerns and allows the ML component to be
developed, tested, and deployed independently.

\subsubsection{RESTful API Design}

Representational State Transfer (REST) is an architectural style for
designing networked applications~\cite{fielding2000}. RESTful APIs use
standard HTTP methods (GET, POST, PUT, PATCH, DELETE) to perform operations
on resources identified by URLs. Key principles include statelessness (each
request contains all information needed to process it), uniform interface,
and client-server separation.

Both the frontend-to-backend and backend-to-AI-service communication in
this thesis follow RESTful conventions. All responses use a consistent
JSON envelope format (\texttt{\{success, data\}} or \texttt{\{success,
error\}}) to simplify client-side error handling.

\subsection{Related Technologies}

\subsubsection{React and Next.js for Frontend Development}

React is a JavaScript library for building user interfaces through a
component-based architecture~\cite{react2024}. Components are reusable,
self-contained units that accept data via props and manage local state
through hooks. React's virtual DOM efficiently updates only the parts of
the page that have changed, resulting in performant rendering even for
data-heavy dashboards.

Next.js is a React framework that adds file-based routing, server-side
rendering, static generation, and API routes on top of
React~\cite{nextjs2024}. This thesis uses Next.js with the App Router for
automatic route generation from the file system, and Turbopack for fast
development builds. TanStack Query (React Query) manages server state,
providing automatic caching, background refetching, and cache invalidation
on mutations.

\subsubsection{Node.js and Express for Backend Services}

Node.js is a JavaScript runtime built on Chrome's V8 engine that enables
server-side JavaScript execution~\cite{nodejs2024}. Its event-driven,
non-blocking I/O model makes it well-suited for handling concurrent API
requests.

Express is a minimal web application framework for Node.js that provides
routing, middleware composition, and HTTP utility
methods~\cite{express2024}. This thesis uses Express~5 with a layered
architecture (routes, controllers, services, repositories) to maintain
separation of concerns. Middleware handles cross-cutting concerns including
authentication (JWT verification), request validation (Zod schemas), and

\subsubsection{FastAPI for Machine Learning Serving}

FastAPI is a modern Python web framework designed for building APIs with
automatic interactive documentation~\cite{fastapi2024}. It uses Python
type hints and Pydantic models for request validation, and supports
asynchronous request handling through ASGI. FastAPI's combination of high
performance, automatic input validation, and auto-generated Swagger
documentation makes it a strong choice for serving machine learning models
as HTTP endpoints. In this thesis, the FastAPI service loads a
scikit-learn model at startup and serves predictions with sub-10ms
response times.

\section{Methodology and System Design}

\subsection{System Architecture Overview}

The system follows a three-tier microservice architecture comprising a
React/Next.js frontend, a Node.js/Express backend, and a Python/FastAPI AI
microservice, supported by MySQL and MongoDB databases. Each tier
communicates via RESTful HTTP APIs using a consistent JSON envelope format.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{02-system-architecture.png}
  \caption{High-level system architecture.}
  \label{fig:system-architecture}
\end{figure}

The separation into three services is motivated by two factors. First,
the machine learning component requires Python and scikit-learn, while
the web backend benefits from JavaScript's rich ecosystem for HTTP
handling, database drivers, and middleware. Second, the AI service can be
developed, tested, and restarted independently without affecting the
main application. The backend supports two modes of operation via an
environment variable: \texttt{AI\_USE\_MOCK=true} uses an in-process mock
formula for development, while \texttt{AI\_USE\_MOCK=false} routes
predictions to the real Python service. This enables independent
development of each component.

\subsection{Database Design}

\subsubsection{MySQL Schema}

MySQL stores all structured relational data. The schema consists of seven
tables with enforced foreign key relationships and cascading deletes.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{03-er-diagram.png}
  \caption{Entity-relationship diagram of the MySQL database.}
  \label{fig:er-diagram}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{users:} Stores authentication credentials (bcrypt-hashed
    passwords), demographics, and role (employee or admin).

  \item \textbf{departments:} A normalised lookup table of factory
    departments (e.g., Assembly Line~A, Maintenance, Quality Control).
    Powers the admin heatmap grouping.

  \item \textbf{hr\_profiles:} One-to-one with \texttt{users}. Contains
    the simulated HR record generated at registration: department
    assignment, designation (1--5), resource allocation (1--10), shift
    type (Day/Night/Rotating), company type, and work-from-home
    availability. Values are drawn from the same ranges as the training
    dataset.

  \item \textbf{assessment\_results:} One row per completed quiz. Stores
    the personal burnout score (0--100), work burnout score (0--100),
    mental fatigue score (0--10), predicted burn rate (0--1), and
    derived risk level. Indexed on \texttt{(user\_id, taken\_at)} for
    efficient trend queries.

  \item \textbf{alerts:} Auto-generated when an assessment crosses a
    risk threshold. Supports three alert types: \texttt{high\_risk},
    \texttt{critical\_risk}, and \texttt{trend\_spike}. Indexed on
    \texttt{(is\_read, created\_at)} for the unread-alert inbox.

  \item \textbf{employee\_kpis:} Monthly performance snapshots per
    employee (attendance rate, productivity score, quality score,
    overtime hours, tasks completed, days absent). Used by admin
    dashboards to correlate burnout risk with workplace performance.

  \item \textbf{help\_resources:} Curated mental health resources with a
    minimum risk level threshold. Only active resources whose threshold
    is at or below the employee's current risk level are displayed.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{MongoDB Collections}

MongoDB stores two collections for document-oriented data that benefits
from schema flexibility:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{cbi\_questions:} A singleton document containing the
    complete CBI quiz definition: quiz name, version number, five
    response options with numeric values, two section definitions
    (personal burnout and work-related burnout), and 13 question objects
    with text, section assignment, and a reverse-scored flag. Storing
    the entire quiz as one document preserves atomicity and eliminates
    joins.

  \item \textbf{quiz\_submissions:} One document per assessment attempt,
    containing the MySQL user ID, quiz version, an array of 13 enriched
    response objects (question ID, answer label, answer value, and
    adjusted value for reverse-scored items), pre-computed section
    averages, and a back-link to the MySQL \texttt{assessment\_results}
    row. This pattern keeps raw answers together as a single atomic unit
    rather than spreading them across multiple relational rows.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Backend Design}

\subsubsection{Layered Architecture}

The Express backend follows a strict four-layer architecture that separates
HTTP concerns from business logic and data access.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{04-backend-layer.png}
  \caption{Layered architecture of the Express backend.}
  \label{fig:backend-layers}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Routes} define HTTP endpoints and attach middleware for
    authentication (\texttt{authenticate}), role enforcement
    (\texttt{requireRole}), and request validation (Zod schemas). Routes
    contain no business logic.

  \item \textbf{Controllers} are thin functions that extract parameters
    from the request, delegate to a service, and wrap the result in the
    standard success envelope \texttt{\{success: true, data\}}.

  \item \textbf{Services} contain all domain logic: CBI scoring with
    reverse scoring, mental fatigue calculation, AI service dispatch,
    risk level mapping, alert rule evaluation, dashboard aggregation,
    and transactional operations.

  \item \textbf{Repositories} own all database interaction. MySQL
    repositories use parameterised SQL via \texttt{mysql2/promise};
    MongoDB repositories use Mongoose schemas. No other layer touches
    the database drivers directly.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Authentication and Authorisation}

The system uses JSON Web Tokens (JWT) for stateless authentication.
Registration hashes the password with bcrypt and issues a signed JWT
containing the user's ID and role. The HR profile is created within the
same MySQL transaction as the user record, ensuring atomicity.

Role-based access control (RBAC) is enforced at the router level. Employee
routes require \texttt{role = ``employee''}, admin routes require
\texttt{role = ``admin''}, and shared routes accept any authenticated user.
The JWT secret must be at least 16 characters, enforced by the environment
variable schema.

\subsubsection{Quiz Scoring Pipeline}

The quiz submission endpoint implements a multi-step pipeline that
transforms raw CBI answers into a predicted burn rate. The pipeline
operates as follows:

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{05-quiz-pipeline.png}
  \caption{Quiz submission scoring pipeline.}
  \label{fig:quiz-pipeline}
\end{figure}

The scoring formulae are:
\begin{align}
  \text{personalBurnoutAvg} &= \frac{1}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{6} v_i
    \quad (0\text{--}100)
  \label{eq:personal} \\
  \text{workBurnoutAvg} &= \frac{1}{7}\sum_{i=7}^{13} v_i
    \quad (0\text{--}100)
  \label{eq:work} \\
  \text{mentalFatigueScore} &=
    \frac{\text{personalBurnoutAvg} + \text{workBurnoutAvg}}{20}
    \quad (0.0\text{--}10.0)
  \label{eq:fatigue}
\end{align}

\noindent where $v_i$ is the adjusted answer value for question~$i$
(with reverse scoring applied to question~10).

\subsubsection{Alert Rules}

Two alert rules are evaluated within the same MySQL transaction that saves
the assessment result:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Threshold alert:} If the risk level is ``high'' or
    ``critical'', an alert of type \texttt{high\_risk} or
    \texttt{critical\_risk} is created.

  \item \textbf{Trend spike alert:} If the current burn rate exceeds the
    previous assessment's burn rate by 0.15 or more, an alert of type
    \texttt{trend\_spike} is created.
\end{enumerate}

Both rules can fire from the same assessment, producing up to two alert
rows.

\subsection{AI Microservice Design}

\subsubsection{Dataset Description}

The machine learning model is trained on the publicly available Kaggle
dataset ``Are Your Employees Burning Out?''~\cite{kaggle2021}, which
contains approximately 22{,}000 employee observations. Each row includes
nine columns, of which three are used as features and one as the target
variable:

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \singlespacing
  \caption{Dataset columns used for model training.}
  \label{tab:dataset-columns}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Column} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Range} & \textbf{Role} \\
    \midrule
    Designation          & Integer & 1--5    & Feature \\
    Resource Allocation  & Integer & 1--10   & Feature (has nulls) \\
    Mental Fatigue Score & Float   & 0.0--10.0 & Feature (has nulls) \\
    Burn Rate            & Float   & 0.0--1.0  & Target variable \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The remaining columns (Employee ID, Date of Joining, Gender, Company Type,
WFH Setup Available) exist in the dataset but are not used for training.
The feature set is intentionally limited to three because these are the
only values available at prediction time in the live application:
designation and resource allocation come from the simulated HR profile,
and mental fatigue score is derived from the CBI quiz.

\subsubsection{Feature Selection Justification}

Keeping the feature set to three serves three purposes. First, it aligns
the model with the data actually collected by the application, avoiding
reliance on features that would require real factory integration. Second,
a smaller feature set produces a more interpretable model, which is
important for both the thesis evaluation and end-user trust. Third, since
the simulated HR values are drawn from the same ranges as the training
data, the model always receives in-distribution inputs, reducing the risk
of unreliable predictions.

\subsubsection{Data Preprocessing}

The preprocessing pipeline consists of three steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Drop null targets:} Rows where the Burn Rate column is
    null are removed, as no training label is available.

  \item \textbf{Median imputation:} Null values in Resource Allocation
    and Mental Fatigue Score are replaced with the column median. Median
    is preferred over mean because it is more robust to outliers on
    bounded ordinal scales.

  \item \textbf{Train/test split:} The dataset is split 80\%/20\% using
    a fixed random seed (\texttt{random\_state=42}) for reproducibility.
\end{enumerate}

At inference time, the Node.js backend coerces any null
\texttt{resource\_allocation} to a default value of~5 (the scale midpoint)
before calling the Python service, ensuring all three inputs are always
present and non-null.

\subsubsection{Model Selection}

Two models are trained and compared:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Linear Regression (baseline):} Provides a performance
    floor and fully transparent learned weights.

  \item \textbf{Random Forest Regressor (primary):} Selected for its
    ability to capture non-linear interactions, robustness to outliers,
    scale invariance, and built-in feature importance.
\end{itemize}

Alternative algorithms (Gradient Boosting, Neural Networks, SVR, kNN)
were considered but not selected. Gradient Boosting (XGBoost/LightGBM)
offers marginal gains over Random Forest for a 3-feature problem while
adding hyperparameter complexity. Neural networks require more data and
tuning to outperform tree-based methods on small tabular datasets. SVR
is sensitive to feature scaling, and kNN degrades with noisy data and
offers no feature importance.

\subsubsection{FastAPI Service Architecture}

The Python AI microservice is a thin HTTP wrapper around the serialised
model:

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{06-ai-service-arch.png}
  \caption{Architecture of the Python/FastAPI AI microservice.}
  \label{fig:ai-service-arch}
\end{figure}

The model file (\texttt{burnout\_model.pkl}) is loaded once during

\subsubsection{Data Flow Pattern}

All server communication follows a consistent four-layer pattern:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Page component} renders the UI and consumes a TanStack
    Query hook.
  \item \textbf{Custom hook} (\texttt{useXxx}) wraps \texttt{useQuery}
    or \texttt{useMutation}, defining cache keys, enabled conditions,
    and invalidation rules.
  \item \textbf{API function} (\texttt{lib/api/}) makes a typed
    \texttt{apiFetch<T>()} call with the endpoint path and parameters.
  \item \textbf{Fetch client} (\texttt{lib/api/client.ts}) prepends the
    base URL, injects the JWT from \texttt{localStorage}, unwraps the
    \texttt{\{success, data\}} envelope, and throws a typed
    \texttt{ApiError} on failure. On 401, it clears the token and
    redirects to login.
\end{enumerate}

Mutations use \texttt{useMutation} with cache invalidation on success,
keeping the UI automatically fresh without full page reloads.

\subsection{Backend Implementation}

\subsubsection{Project Structure and Configuration}

The backend project follows the folder structure described in
Section~3.3.1, with clear separation between configuration, middleware,
models, services, controllers, routes, validators, and utilities. All
environment variables are validated at startup using a Zod schema in
\texttt{config/env.js}; the process exits immediately with a descriptive
error message if any required variable is missing or malformed,
guaranteeing that the server never starts in a half-configured state.

Database connections are established in sequence during startup:
\texttt{server.js} first pings MySQL, then connects to MongoDB, then
starts the HTTP listener. A failure at any step aborts the process.
Graceful shutdown is handled via SIGINT/SIGTERM handlers that close the
HTTP server followed by both database connections.

\subsubsection{Authentication Flow Implementation}

Registration creates a user record and a synthetic HR profile within a
single MySQL transaction:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Validate the request body with Zod (email, password, name,
    gender, date of birth).
  \item Hash the password with bcrypt (configurable salt rounds).
  \item Insert the user row into the \texttt{users} table.
  \item Generate a simulated HR profile: assign a random department,
    generate designation (1--5), resource allocation (1--10), shift type,
    company type, WFH availability, and date of joining.
  \item Insert the HR profile into \texttt{hr\_profiles}.
  \item Sign a JWT containing the user's ID and role; return the token
    and user object.
\end{enumerate}

The transaction wrapper ensures that a failure during HR profile generation
does not leave an orphaned user record. Login validates credentials against
the bcrypt hash and issues a JWT with a configurable expiration (default
7~days).

\subsubsection{Quiz Scoring Implementation}

The quiz submission handler in \texttt{quiz.service.js} implements the
full pipeline described in Section~3.3.3. Key implementation details
include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Answer validation:} The service verifies that exactly 13
    answers are submitted, all question IDs are valid, there are no
    duplicate IDs, and all answer values are from the allowed set
    \{0, 25, 50, 75, 100\}.

  \item \textbf{Reverse scoring:} Question~10 (``Do you have enough
    energy for family and friends during leisure time?'') is
    reverse-scored: \texttt{adjustedValue = 100 - rawValue}. The
    enriched response object records both the raw and adjusted values.

  \item \textbf{MongoDB-first persistence:} The raw submission is saved
    to MongoDB before the AI call, ensuring answers are never lost even
    if subsequent steps fail. On failure, the orphan document is cleaned
    up.

  \item \textbf{Transactional MySQL write:} The assessment result
    insertion and alert rule evaluation occur within a single MySQL
    transaction. The MongoDB document is then back-linked with the MySQL
    assessment ID.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Dashboard Aggregation}

The admin dashboard endpoint computes company-wide and per-department
statistics in a single API call. The latest assessment per employee is
determined using a correlated subquery that selects the most recent
\texttt{assessment\_results.id} for each user. This result set is then
aggregated in JavaScript to produce:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Company overview: total employees, total assessments, average
    burn rate, and risk distribution counts.
  \item Per-department breakdown: employee count, average burn rate,
    high-risk count, and risk distribution.
  \item Department KPIs: average composite KPI per department
    (mean of attendance, productivity, and quality scores).
  \item Recent alerts: the 10 most recent alert records.
\end{itemize}

The department drill-down endpoint adds sorting, filtering (by risk level,
shift type, and designation), and server-side pagination. Employees are
displayed using anonymous IDs (``Worker \#0001'') to satisfy the privacy
requirement.

\subsubsection{Alert System Implementation}

The alert service evaluates two rules after every assessment, as described
in Section~3.3.4. Both the threshold check and the trend spike comparison
execute within the same MySQL transaction that wrote the assessment result.
The trend spike threshold (default 0.15) is configurable via the
\texttt{ALERT\_TREND\_SPIKE\_DELTA} environment variable.

\subsubsection{FastAPI Prediction Endpoint}

The prediction endpoint validates inputs using Pydantic models that enforce
strict ranges: designation (1--5), resource allocation (1--10), and mental
fatigue score (0--10). The model is loaded once at startup via a lifespan
handler and cached in memory. Each prediction call constructs a feature
array, runs \texttt{model.predict()}, clamps the result to the [0,~1]
range, and returns the burn rate in the standard envelope format. Response
times are consistently under 10~milliseconds.

\subsubsection{Integration with Express Backend}

The Express backend's \texttt{ai.service.js} implements a dual-mode
architecture controlled by the \texttt{AI\_USE\_MOCK} environment variable.
When set to \texttt{false}, the service sends an HTTP POST to the Python
endpoint with the three features and parses
\texttt{response.data.predictedBurnRate}. The request uses an
\texttt{AbortController} with a configurable timeout
(\texttt{AI\_SERVICE\_TIMEOUT\_MS}); timeouts and non-2xx responses are
wrapped as \texttt{ApiError.internal()} and surfaced to the frontend as a
generic 500 error.

When set to \texttt{true}, a mock formula is used in-process:
\begin{equation}
  \text{burnRate} = \text{clamp}_{[0,1]}\!\left(
    0.07 \cdot \text{mfs} + 0.03 \cdot d + 0.02 \cdot ra - 0.05
    + \text{noise}
  \right)
  \label{eq:mock-formula}
\end{equation}

\noindent where \textit{mfs} is the mental fatigue score, \textit{d} is
designation, \textit{ra} is resource allocation, and \textit{noise} is
uniform random in $[\!-0.025, +0.025]$. Switching between modes requires
no code changes---only an environment variable update and a server restart.

Custom hooks in \texttt{lib/hooks/} wrap \texttt{useQuery} for read
operations and \texttt{useMutation} for write operations. Mutations
automatically invalidate related query keys on success, ensuring the UI
stays synchronised with the server without manual refetch calls.

blocks), error states display inline messages in crimson-tinted cards, and
empty data sets show an \texttt{EmptyState} component with an icon,

\section{Discussion and Evaluation}

\subsection{Model Performance Analysis}

The Random Forest model achieves an $R^{2}$ of 0.90, an MAE of 0.048, and
an RMSE of 0.061 on the held-out test set
(Table~\ref{tab:model-comparison}). These results indicate that the model
explains 90\% of the variance in burn rate and is typically accurate to
within 5 percentage points on the 0--1 scale. The 3.6 percentage-point
improvement in $R^{2}$ over Linear Regression demonstrates that the
Random Forest captures meaningful non-linear interactions between the three
features---interactions that a linear model cannot represent without manual
feature engineering.

Feature importance analysis reveals that mental fatigue score accounts for
approximately 85\% of the model's predictive power, with resource
allocation contributing around 10\% and designation approximately 5\%.
This distribution is consistent with domain knowledge: an employee's
psychological state is the most direct indicator of burnout risk, while
workload intensity and seniority play secondary roles. The strong
dominance of mental fatigue score also validates the system's architecture,
which places the CBI quiz---the source of the mental fatigue score---at
the centre of the assessment workflow.

The residual analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:residual-analysis}) shows that
prediction errors are approximately randomly distributed around zero with
no systematic pattern, indicating that the model does not exhibit
consistent over- or under-prediction across the burn rate range. The
residual distribution is approximately normal, centred at zero, which is a
desirable property for a regression model.

Compared to public Kaggle notebooks that train on the same dataset with
all available features, the system's $R^{2}$ of 0.90 using only three
features falls within the reported range of 0.90--0.93 for Random Forest
models. This suggests that the three selected features capture the
majority of the predictive signal, and the additional columns (Gender,
Company Type, WFH Setup) contribute relatively little to burn rate
prediction.

\subsection{Answering the Research Questions}

\textbf{RQ1: How effectively can a Random Forest regression model predict
employee burn rate using only three input features?}

The Random Forest model achieves an $R^{2}$ of 0.90 with an MAE of 0.048,
demonstrating that three features---designation, resource allocation, and
mental fatigue score---are sufficient for effective burn rate prediction.
The model's performance is comparable to published results that use the
full feature set, indicating that the three selected features capture the
dominant signal in the data.

\textbf{RQ2: Can a web-based system provide actionable burnout insights to
both employees and HR administrators?}

The employee portal provides personalised burn rate gauges, historical
trend charts, section score breakdowns, and contextual help resources,
enabling workers to monitor their own mental health proactively. The admin
portal provides department heatmaps, risk distribution charts,
KPI-vs-burn-rate correlations, and automated alerts, giving HR managers
actionable visibility into organisational burnout patterns. The functional
testing results confirm that all data flows correctly from quiz submission
through prediction to dashboard visualisation.

\textbf{RQ3: What are the practical benefits and trade-offs of using a
microservice architecture to separate ML inference from the application
backend?}

The microservice architecture provides three concrete benefits:
(1)~technology heterogeneity---Python's scikit-learn ecosystem for ML and
JavaScript's Express ecosystem for web logic are each used where they excel;
(2)~independent development---the AI service can be retrained and redeployed
without modifying or restarting the backend; (3)~the mock/real switch
(\texttt{AI\_USE\_MOCK}) enables fully functional development without running
the Python service. The primary trade-off is increased operational
complexity: two servers must be running and communicating, and network
failures between them require timeout handling and error propagation.

\subsection{Comparison with Related Work}

As summarised in Table~\ref{tab:related-comparison}, this system is the

This thesis fills the integration gap by unifying psychometric assessment,
machine learning prediction, and multi-level analytics into a single
platform that serves both individual workers and organisational decision
makers.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

\subsection{Conclusion}

This thesis presented the design, implementation, and evaluation of an
intelligent web-based framework for the early detection of employee burnout
in industrial workplaces. The system integrates a validated psychometric
instrument (the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory), a machine learning
prediction model (Random Forest regression), and a full-stack web
application with role-based portals for both employees and administrators.

The Random Forest model, trained on approximately 22{,}000 records from a
public dataset, achieves an $R^{2}$ of 0.90 using only three input
features, demonstrating that effective burn rate prediction is possible
from a minimal feature set. The employee portal empowers workers with
self-monitoring tools---burn rate gauges, trend charts, and contextual
support resources---while the admin portal provides organisational-level
visibility through department heatmaps, risk distribution analytics, KPI
correlations, and automated alerts.

The microservice architecture successfully separates the machine learning
concern (Python/FastAPI) from the web application backend
(Node.js/Express), enabling independent development and deployment.
End-to-end functional testing confirmed that all core workflows operate
correctly, from quiz submission and scoring through AI prediction to
dashboard visualisation and alert generation.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations should be acknowledged:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulated HR data.} Designation and resource allocation
    values are randomly generated at registration rather than sourced
    from a real factory HR system. While values are drawn from the
    training dataset's ranges, they do not reflect actual workplace
    conditions.

  \item \textbf{Limited feature set.} The model uses only three
    features. Including additional signals such as actual shift
    patterns, overtime records, or longitudinal CBI trends could improve
    predictive accuracy.

  \item \textbf{Single dataset.} Model training and evaluation rely on a
    single public Kaggle dataset. The model's performance may not
    generalise across different industries, cultures, or organisational
    contexts.

  \item \textbf{Simulated KPI data.} The employee KPI records
    (attendance, productivity, quality) are seeded rather than collected
    from real workplace systems, limiting the validity of the
    KPI-vs-burn-rate correlation analysis.

  \item \textbf{Desktop-only interface.} The application is designed for
    minimum 992~px screen widths. Factory workers using personal mobile
    devices cannot access the system.

  \item \textbf{No formal usability study.} The system was tested
    functionally but not evaluated through a structured usability study
    with real end users.

  \item \textbf{No real-time updates.} The admin dashboard uses
    polling-based data fetching via TanStack Query rather than real-time
    push notifications via WebSocket or Server-Sent Events.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{subsec:future}

The following directions are proposed for future development:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mobile responsive design.} Extend the frontend with
    responsive breakpoints to support mobile and tablet access, enabling
    factory workers to complete assessments on personal devices.

  \item \textbf{Real-time alerts.} Implement WebSocket or Server-Sent
    Events to push new alerts to the admin dashboard instantly, without
    requiring page refresh or polling intervals.

  \item \textbf{Integration with real HR systems.} Replace the simulated
    HR profile generator with connectors to actual HR information
    systems (e.g., SAP SuccessFactors, BambooHR) to provide authentic
    designation and workload data.

  \item \textbf{Advanced ML models.} Explore Gradient Boosting
    (XGBoost, LightGBM) and time-series models that incorporate
    longitudinal CBI trends, enabling the system to predict burn rate
    trajectories rather than point estimates.

  \item \textbf{Expanded feature engineering.} Incorporate additional

\section*{Appendix C: Copenhagen Burnout Inventory Questionnaire}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Appendix C: CBI Questionnaire}

The system uses 13 questions from the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory,
covering two sections. Response options for all questions are: Always
(100), Often (75), Sometimes (50), Seldom (25), Never/almost never (0).

\subsection*{C.1 Personal Burnout (Questions 1--6)}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item How often do you feel tired?
  \item How often are you physically exhausted?
  \item How often are you emotionally exhausted?
  \item How often do you think: ``I can't take it anymore''?
  \item How often do you feel worn out?
  \item How often do you feel weak and susceptible to illness?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{C.2 Work-Related Burnout (Questions 7--13)}

\begin{enumerate}
  \setcounter{enumi}{6}
  \item Do you feel worn out at the end of the working day?
  \item Are you exhausted in the morning at the thought of another day
    at work?
  \item Do you feel that every working hour is tiring for you?
  \item Do you have enough energy for family and friends during leisure
    time? \textit{(Reverse-scored: adjustedValue = 100 $-$ rawValue)}
  \item Is your work emotionally exhausting?
  \item Does your work frustrate you?
  \item Do you feel burnt out because of your work?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{C.3 Scoring}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Calculate the personal burnout average: mean of questions 1--6
    (range 0--100).
  \item Calculate the work-related burnout average: mean of questions
    7--13 (range 0--100), with question~10 reverse-scored.
  \item Calculate the mental fatigue score:
    $\text{mentalFatigueScore} = \frac{\text{personalAvg} +
    \text{workAvg}}{20}$ (range 0.0--10.0).
\end{enumerate}
