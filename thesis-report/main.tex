\begin{center}
  \textbf{ABSTRACT}
\end{center}

\vspace{1cm}

Occupational burnout imposes measurable costs on industrial workplaces through absenteeism, reduced output, and elevated accident rates. Conventional detection methods rely on periodic surveys or supervisor observation, both of which are inherently reactive: by the time burnout becomes visible, its organisational impact has already accumulated.

This thesis presents the design and implementation of an intelligent web-based framework for early burnout detection in industrial settings. The system integrates a validated psychometric instrument, the \textbf{Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI)}, with a \textbf{Random Forest} regression model that predicts a continuous burnout score from three features: employee designation, resource allocation, and a mental fatigue score derived from CBI responses.

The framework follows a microservice architecture comprising three components. A \textbf{React/Next.js} frontend provides role-differentiated portals for employees (self-assessment, trend tracking, support resources) and administrators (department heatmaps, risk distribution analytics, KPI correlation charts, automated alert inbox). A \textbf{Node.js/Express} backend manages authentication, quiz scoring, risk classification, alert generation, and dashboard aggregation, backed by \textbf{MySQL} for relational data and \textbf{MongoDB} for the quiz definition and raw submissions. A \textbf{Python/FastAPI} microservice loads the serialised model at startup and exposes a prediction endpoint, maintaining a clean separation between the machine learning component and the application backend.

The Random Forest model was trained on approximately 22{,}000 records from a public Kaggle dataset. On the held-out test set, it achieves an $R^{2}$ of 0.90, an MAE of 0.048, and an RMSE of 0.061, outperforming the Linear Regression baseline across all three metrics. Feature importance analysis indicates that the mental fatigue score accounts for approximately 81\% of predictive weight, consistent with its role as the primary psychometric input.

Several limitations apply. HR profile data for designation and resource allocation are synthetically generated rather than sourced from a real factory system. The model relies on three features and is evaluated against a single public dataset, which constrains generalisability across industries and organisational contexts. No formal usability study was conducted. Within these constraints, the system demonstrates a practical architecture for combining psychometric assessment, machine learning prediction, and organisational analytics into a unified platform for proactive burnout management.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} framework, employee's mental health, employee burnout detection, Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, Random Forest regression, Linear regression, microservice architecture, industrial workforce, web-based health monitoring.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}

Industrial workers operate under conditions that make occupational burnout particularly prevalent. Factory environments routinely combine physically demanding tasks, rigid shift schedules, production quotas, and limited autonomy. The World Health Organisation recognised burnout as an occupational phenomenon in the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), defining it as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed~\cite{who2019}. Its three characteristic dimensions, emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, and reduced personal accomplishment, manifest in measurable organisational outcomes: declining productivity, increased absenteeism, elevated turnover, and higher healthcare costs~\cite{maslach2001}.

Stress-related conditions, including burnout, are estimated to cost organisations hundreds of billions of dollars annually in lost productivity and medical expenses~\cite{gallup2024}. In manufacturing and industrial sectors the consequences extend beyond individual welfare: fatigued workers are disproportionately associated with safety incidents, quality defects, and equipment damage. Despite these risks, most organisations still rely on ad-hoc supervisor observation and infrequent surveys to assess employee mental health, an approach that is inherently reactive rather than preventive.

Advances in machine learning and web development have created practical opportunities to shift burnout management toward proactive, continuous monitoring. By pairing validated psychometric instruments with predictive models and real-time dashboards, it is feasible to build systems that estimate burnout risk at both the individual and organisational level, alerting management before problems escalate while also equipping employees with self-monitoring tools.

\subsection{Motivating Scenario and Use Cases}

Consider a mid-sized manufacturing plant with 200 workers across six departments operating rotating day and night shifts. With three HR staff managing attendance, grievances, and benefits concurrently, proactive mental health monitoring is not feasible under current practice. Burnout is only surfaced when a supervisor notices persistent signs such as chronic fatigue or declining output and escalates to HR. At that point, weeks or months of deteriorating mental health have already passed and the department has likely absorbed measurable productivity losses.

The system proposed in this thesis replaces this reactive workflow with a continuous, data-driven one. Two use cases drive the design:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Employee self-assessment and trend tracking.} Each worker periodically completes a 13-question burnout assessment through a web portal. The system immediately scores the quiz, invokes the ML prediction endpoint, and displays the predicted burn rate as a gauge visualization. Historical results are plotted as trend charts, allowing the worker to monitor changes over time. When risk is classified as high or critical, curated mental health resources are surfaced on the results page.

  \item \textbf{HR department-level monitoring and alerts.} The admin portal aggregates assessment data across the workforce. A department heatmap visualises average burn rates by organisational unit, enabling HR to identify high-stress areas. Automated alerts are generated when an individual's burn rate exceeds a critical threshold or increases sharply between consecutive assessments. Drill-down views present anonymised employee-level detail, balancing privacy with the need for targeted intervention.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Despite the well-documented impact of burnout on industrial productivity and worker welfare, several gaps remain in current organisational practice:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Detection is reactive.} Most organisations identify burnout only after its symptoms, including absenteeism, errors, and resignations, have already materialised. No systematic early-warning mechanism is in place.

  \item \textbf{Psychometric data is underutilised.} Validated instruments such as the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory are freely available, yet they are rarely embedded in continuous monitoring workflows. When surveys are administered, results are typically analysed manually and in isolation rather than fed into an integrated system.

  \item \textbf{No predictive capability.} Traditional approaches report current symptom levels but do not estimate future risk. Machine learning can address this gap by learning patterns from historical data and projecting burn rates from a small set of measurable inputs.

  \item \textbf{No organisational-level visibility.} Individual survey results remain isolated. HR managers lack aggregated analytical tools, such as department heatmaps, risk distribution charts, and trend analyses, that would support data-driven workforce management decisions.
\end{enumerate}

This thesis addresses these gaps through an integrated web-based framework that combines psychometric assessment, machine learning prediction, and multi-level analytics on a single platform.

\subsection{Scope and Objectives}

The scope of this thesis covers the design, implementation, and evaluation of a desktop web application for early burnout detection in industrial workplaces. The system uses simulated HR data (since real factory records are private and inaccessible), the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory as the psychometric tool, and a Random Forest regression model trained on a public dataset as the predictive engine.

The specific objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Implement a web-based Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI) assessment that turns employee responses into a mental fatigue score ready for machine learning input.

  \item Train and evaluate a Random Forest regression model to predict employee burn rate from three features: designation, resource allocation, and mental fatigue score.

  \item Build an employee self-monitoring portal with a burn rate gauge, historical trend charts, section score breakdowns, and contextual access to mental health resources.

  \item Build an administrative command center with department heatmaps, risk distribution analytics, KPI correlation views, and a drill-down capability for individual employee analysis.

  \item Implement an automated alert system that notifies administrators when employees cross critical risk thresholds or show sharp burn rate increases between assessments.

  \item Demonstrate a microservice architecture that cleanly separates the machine learning inference part (Python/FastAPI) from the application backend (Node.js/Express) and the user interface (React/Next.js).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research Questions}

This thesis investigates the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1.}] How effectively can a Random Forest regression
    model predict employee burn rate using only three input features
    (designation, resource allocation, and mental fatigue score)?

  \item[\textbf{RQ2.}] Can a web-based system provide actionable burnout
    insights to both employees (through self-monitoring tools) and HR
    administrators (through department-level analytics and automated
    alerts)?

  \item[\textbf{RQ3.}] What are the practical benefits and trade-offs of
    using a microservice architecture to separate machine learning
    inference from the application backend in a health monitoring
    system?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Assumptions and Proposed Solution}

The following assumptions underlie the design of the system:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulated HR data.} Real factory HR databases are
    private and inaccessible for academic research. The backend
    generates synthetic HR profiles (designation, resource allocation,
    department, shift type) at registration time, drawing values from
    the same ranges as the training dataset to ensure the ML model
    receives in-distribution inputs.

  \item \textbf{CBI as a valid burnout measure.} The Copenhagen Burnout
    Inventory is an established, freely available instrument with
    demonstrated reliability and validity in occupational health
    research. Its 13-question excerpt (personal and work-related
    burnout sections) is used as the sole actively collected data
    point.

  \item \textbf{Desktop-only usage.} The system targets HR office
    terminals and factory kiosk stations. No mobile-responsive layout
    is implemented.

  \item \textbf{Single-language interface.} The user interface is
    presented in English. Multi-language support is deferred to future
    work.
\end{itemize}

The proposed solution is a three-tier web app:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{React/Next.js frontend} with two role-based portals (employee and admin), connected to the backend through RESTful API calls, with TanStack Query handling server state.

  \item A \textbf{Node.js/Express backend} that manages authentication (JWT), quiz scoring (CBI with reverse scoring), risk classification, alert generation, dashboard aggregation, and resource management, using MySQL and MongoDB as databases.

  \item A \textbf{Python/FastAPI AI microservice} that loads a pre-trained Random Forest model at startup and exposes a single prediction endpoint. The backend can switch between the real AI service and an in-process mock formula through an environment variable, so each component can be developed and deployed independently.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Occupational Burnout}

\subsubsection{Definition and Impact on the Industrial Workforce}

The concept of occupational burnout was first introduced by Freudenberger
in 1974 to describe the state of physical and emotional depletion observed
among human service workers~\cite{freudenberger1974}. The construct was
later formalised by Maslach and Jackson, who defined burnout as a
three-dimensional syndrome comprising emotional exhaustion,
depersonalisation (cynicism), and reduced personal
accomplishment~\cite{maslach1981}. In 2019 the World Health Organization
included burnout in the ICD-11 as an occupational phenomenon, describing it
as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been
successfully managed~\cite{who2019}.

In industrial and manufacturing environments, the consequences of burnout
extend beyond individual suffering. Research has linked burnout to
increased absenteeism, higher employee turnover, reduced product quality,
and elevated workplace accident rates~\cite{ahola2017}. A Gallup
meta-analysis across industries found that employees experiencing burnout
are 63\% more likely to take a sick day, 2.6 times more likely to seek a
new job, and 13\% less confident in their performance~\cite{gallup2024}.
For factory settings with tight production schedules and safety-critical processes, these numbers translate directly into operational and financial risk.

\subsubsection{Burnout Measurement Instruments}

The two most widely used instruments for measuring occupational burnout are the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) and the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI).

The \textbf{Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI)}, developed in 1981~\cite{maslach1981}, is the most cited burnout measure in the literature. It has 22 items across three subscales (emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment). However, the MBI is copyrighted and requires a commercial licence, which limits its adoption in academic and open-source projects.

The \textbf{Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI)}, developed by Kristensen et~al.\ in 2005~\cite{kristensen2005}, was created as a freely available alternative. The CBI sees burnout as a state of physical and psychological fatigue and exhaustion, measured along three dimensions: personal burnout, work-related burnout, and client-related burnout. Each dimension is assessed through a set of questions scored on a five-point frequency scale ranging from ``Always'' (100 points) to ``Never/almost never'' (0 points). The CBI has shown high internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha exceeding 0.85 across all subscales) and strong predictive validity for outcomes such as sickness absence and sleep disturbance~\cite{kristensen2005}.

\subsubsection{The Copenhagen Burnout Inventory in This Thesis}

The CBI was selected for this thesis on three grounds. First, it is freely available without licensing restrictions, which makes it appropriate for academic and open-source use where the MBI is not. Second, the instrument's distinction between personal and work-related burnout enables more granular feedback to both employees and administrators. Third, the frequency-based response scale produces a numeric score that integrates naturally with the machine learning pipeline without additional transformation.

As the target population consists of industrial workers rather than client-facing professionals, the client-related subscale is omitted. The system uses the remaining 13 questions: six for personal burnout and seven for work-related burnout. One item, ``Do you have enough energy for family and friends during leisure time?'', is reverse-scored because an affirmative response indicates lower burnout. Section averages are combined and normalised to a 0--10 scale to produce the mental fatigue score used as input to the model.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Health Prediction}

\subsubsection{Supervised Regression}

Machine learning tasks are broadly categorised into supervised and
unsupervised learning. In supervised learning, the algorithm is trained on
a labelled dataset where each input is associated with a known
output~\cite{bishop2006}. When the output is a continuous numeric value,
the task is called regression.

Burnout prediction fits naturally into the supervised regression paradigm:
the training dataset contains employee observations with a known burn rate
(the target variable), and the goal is to learn a function that maps
employee features to a predicted burn rate for new, unseen employees.

\subsubsection{Linear Regression}

Linear Regression models the relationship between inputs and target as a linear combination:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{y} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + w_3 x_3 + b
  \label{eq:linear-regression}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $x_1, x_2,$ and $x_3$ are designation, resource allocation, and mental fatigue score, $w_1, w_2, w_3$ are learned weights, and $b$ is the intercept. Parameters are estimated by minimising the sum of squared residuals~\cite{hastie2009}.

Linear Regression serves as the baseline in this thesis because its simplicity establishes a performance floor and its learned coefficients are directly interpretable. A large weight on the mental fatigue score, for example, immediately indicates that fatigue dominates the prediction. The primary limitation is its assumption of linearity, which may not hold when feature interactions are present.

\subsubsection{Random Forest Regression}

Random Forest is an ensemble method introduced by Breiman~\cite{breiman2001} that constructs a collection of decision trees. Each tree is trained on a bootstrap sample of the data, and each split considers a random subset of features. Predictions are obtained by averaging outputs across all trees.

Random Forest was selected as the primary model for several reasons:

\begin{itemize}
  \item It captures non-linear relationships and feature interactions without manual feature engineering.
  \item It is robust to outliers, since individual trees use threshold-based splits rather than a global fit to all data points.
  \item It is invariant to feature scale, so designation (1--5) and resource allocation (1--10) do not require normalisation.
  \item It provides built-in feature importance scores, which support both model interpretation and design validation.
\end{itemize}

For tabular datasets with a moderate number of features and tens of thousands of rows, Random Forest generally achieves strong performance with minimal hyperparameter tuning, making it suitable for a thesis-scope project~\cite{fernandez2014}. A limitation is that it produces point estimates rather than prediction intervals, which constrains confidence quantification.

\subsubsection{Model Evaluation Metrics}

Three complementary metrics are used to evaluate the regression models:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE):} The average absolute difference between predicted and actual values. On a 0--1 burn rate scale, an MAE of 0.05 indicates that predictions are typically within 5 percentage points of the true value.

  \item \textbf{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):} The square root of the mean squared error. By squaring residuals before averaging, RMSE penalises large errors more heavily than MAE, making it sensitive to outlier predictions.

  \item \textbf{Coefficient of Determination ($R^{2}$):} The proportion of variance in the target explained by the model. An $R^{2}$ of 0.90 indicates that 90\% of burn rate variance is accounted for by the three input features.
\end{itemize}

Reporting all three metrics provides a more complete picture than any single measure. MAE reflects typical prediction accuracy, RMSE captures worst-case sensitivity, and $R^{2}$ summarises overall explanatory power~\cite{hastie2009}.

\subsection{Web Application Architectures}

\subsubsection{Microservices vs.\ Monolithic Architecture}

A monolithic architecture co-locates all application concerns, including the user interface, business logic, data access, and any ML components, within a single deployable unit. This approach simplifies initial setup but becomes increasingly difficult to maintain as the application grows, particularly when different subsystems require heterogeneous technology stacks~\cite{newman2015}.

A microservice architecture decomposes the application into independently deployable services, each responsible for a well-defined boundary of functionality~\cite{newman2015}. Services communicate over HTTP or lightweight messaging. The main advantages are independent deployment, technology heterogeneity, and fault isolation: a failure in one service does not propagate to others. The trade-off is increased operational complexity, as multiple services must be deployed, networked, and monitored.

A microservice architecture was selected for this thesis because the machine learning component has fundamentally different runtime requirements from the web backend. The AI service requires Python and scikit-learn, while the backend is more naturally implemented in Node.js. Separating them into a FastAPI microservice and an Express backend allows each to be developed, tested, and redeployed independently. The trade-off is that both services must be running simultaneously, and network failures between them require explicit timeout handling and error propagation logic.

\subsubsection{RESTful API Design}

Representational State Transfer (REST) defines a set of architectural constraints for distributed hypermedia systems~\cite{fielding2000}. RESTful APIs use standard HTTP methods (GET, POST, PUT, PATCH, DELETE) mapped to resource URLs. Key constraints include statelessness, meaning each request must contain all information necessary to process it, a uniform interface, and client-server separation.

Both the frontend-to-backend and backend-to-AI-service communication in this project follow REST conventions. All responses use a uniform JSON envelope, either \texttt{\{success: true, data: \{...\}\}} or \texttt{\{success: false, error: \{...\}\}}, which simplifies error handling on the client side.

\subsection{Related Technologies}

\subsubsection{React and Next.js for Frontend Development}

React is a component-based JavaScript library for building user interfaces~\cite{react2024}. Components receive data through props and manage local state via hooks. React's virtual DOM diffing algorithm minimises direct DOM manipulation, which keeps rendering efficient even in data-heavy dashboard views.

Next.js extends React with file-system-based routing, server-side rendering, and built-in API route support~\cite{nextjs2024}. This thesis uses Next.js~16 with the App Router, where the folder structure maps directly to application routes, and Turbopack for fast development rebuilds. Server state, including caching, background refetching, and cache invalidation on mutations, is handled by TanStack Query (React Query), which removes the need for manual fetch management across components.

\subsubsection{Node.js and Express for Backend Services}

Node.js is a JavaScript runtime built on the V8 engine that enables server-side execution of JavaScript~\cite{nodejs2024}. Its non-blocking, event-driven model allows it to handle a large number of concurrent API requests without dedicated threads per connection.

Express is a minimal web framework for Node.js that provides routing, middleware chaining, and HTTP request handling~\cite{express2024}. The backend is implemented with Express~5 and organised into four layers (routes, controllers, services, repositories) to keep responsibilities separated. Cross-cutting concerns such as authentication (JWT verification), input validation (Zod schemas), and error handling are applied as middleware across all routes.

\subsubsection{FastAPI for Machine Learning Serving}

FastAPI was selected for the AI microservice because of its high throughput, automatic OpenAPI documentation generation, and strict input validation via Pydantic~\cite{fastapi2024}. Its asynchronous request handling is well-suited to a service that performs CPU-bound inference and must remain responsive under concurrent requests. In this project, the FastAPI service loads the serialised scikit-learn model at startup and returns predictions in under 10 milliseconds per request.

\subsection{Related Work}

\section{Methodology and System Design}

\subsection{System Architecture Overview}

The system follows a three-service microservice design comprising a React/Next.js frontend, a Node.js/Express backend, and a Python/FastAPI AI service. Relational data is stored in MySQL and document data in MongoDB. All three services communicate over HTTP using a consistent JSON envelope format.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{02-system-architecture.png}
  \caption{High-level system architecture.}
  \label{fig:system-architecture}
\end{figure}

The machine learning component was separated into an independent service because its runtime requirements differ fundamentally from the web backend. Python and scikit-learn are required for inference, while the backend is better suited to the Node.js ecosystem given its extensive middleware and database tooling. Separating the AI component into a standalone FastAPI service also improves maintainability: the model can be retrained and redeployed without touching the main application. A configurable environment variable (\texttt{AI\_USE\_MOCK}) allows the backend to switch between the real Python service and an in-process mock formula during development, enabling the frontend and backend to be built and tested independently.

\subsection{Database Design}

\subsubsection{MySQL Schema}

MySQL stores all relational, structured data. The schema consists of seven tables linked by foreign keys with cascading deletes to maintain referential integrity.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{03-er-diagram.png}
  \caption{Entity-relationship diagram of the MySQL database.}
  \label{fig:er-diagram}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{users:} Stores authentication credentials (bcrypt-hashed passwords), basic demographic fields, and role assignment (employee or admin).

  \item \textbf{departments:} A reference table of factory organisational units (e.g.\ Assembly Line A, Maintenance, Quality Control), used to group employees for heatmap aggregation.

  \item \textbf{hr\_profiles:} A one-to-one extension of the users table containing the synthetic HR record generated at registration: department, designation (1--5), resource allocation (1--10), shift type, company type, and WFH availability. Values are drawn from the same ranges as the training dataset to ensure in-distribution inputs to the model.

  \item \textbf{assessment\_results:} Records each completed assessment with personal burnout score (0--100), work burnout score (0--100), mental fatigue score (0--10), predicted burn rate (0--1), and risk level. A composite index on \texttt{(user\_id, taken\_at)} supports efficient trend queries.

  \item \textbf{alerts:} Stores system-generated notifications of types \texttt{high\_risk}, \texttt{critical\_risk}, and \texttt{trend\_spike}. An index on \texttt{(is\_read, created\_at)} supports the unread inbox query.

  \item \textbf{employee\_kpis:} Monthly per-employee performance snapshots covering attendance, productivity, quality, overtime, task completion, and absence days. The admin dashboard uses this table to correlate KPI trends with burn rate.

  \item \textbf{help\_resources:} Mental health resources, each assigned a minimum risk level. Only resources at or below the employee's current risk level are surfaced, avoiding irrelevant recommendations.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{MongoDB Collections}

MongoDB was selected for two collections whose document structure would be inefficient to represent in a relational schema:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{cbi\_questions:} A singleton document containing the complete CBI quiz definition, including response options, section groupings, and per-question metadata (section membership, reverse-scoring flag). Storing the entire quiz as one document allows the backend to fetch the complete quiz in a single read, avoiding a multi-table join.

  \item \textbf{quiz\_submissions:} One document per completed assessment, recording the MySQL user ID, quiz version, all 13 individual responses with raw and adjusted values, section averages, and a back-reference to the MySQL assessment ID. The nested structure keeps a user's full response set together as a single unit, which is better suited to document storage than normalised relational tables.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Backend Design}

\subsubsection{Layered Architecture}

The Express backend follows a strict four-layer architecture to ensure that HTTP handling, business logic, and data access remain decoupled.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{04-backend-layer.png}
  \caption{Layered architecture of the Express backend.}
  \label{fig:backend-layers}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Routes:} Map URL paths to middleware chains. Each route applies authentication (\texttt{authenticate}), role enforcement (\texttt{requireRole}), and input validation (Zod schemas) before delegating to a controller. No business logic is present at this layer.

  \item \textbf{Controllers:} Extract parameters from the request, delegate to the appropriate service method, and wrap the result in the standard \texttt{\{success: true, data\}} envelope before sending the response.

  \item \textbf{Services:} Contain all business logic: CBI scoring, mental fatigue calculation, AI prediction calls, risk classification, alert rule evaluation, dashboard aggregation, and transactional write coordination.

  \item \textbf{Repositories:} Encapsulate all database access. MySQL queries are executed via parameterised \texttt{mysql2/promise} calls; MongoDB access uses Mongoose. No other layer interacts with the database directly.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Authentication and Authorisation}

Authentication is implemented with JSON Web Tokens (JWT). During registration, the password is hashed with bcrypt and a user record and synthetic HR profile are created within a single MySQL transaction, ensuring that no orphaned user record is left if HR profile generation fails. The issued JWT contains the user ID and role and is signed with a secret key enforced to be at least 16 characters.

Route protection is applied through middleware: employee-only routes require \texttt{role = ``employee''}, admin-only routes require \texttt{role = ``admin''}, and shared routes require only a valid token. JWT forgery is mitigated by the signing key requirement.

\subsubsection{Quiz Scoring Pipeline}

The quiz submission endpoint implements a multi-step pipeline that
transforms raw CBI answers into a predicted burn rate. The pipeline
operates as follows:

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{05-quiz-pipeline.png}
  \caption{Submitting Quiz results pipeline.}
  \label{fig:quiz-pipeline}
\end{figure}

The scoring formulae are:
\begin{align}
  \text{personalBurnoutAvg} &= \frac{1}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{6} v_i
    \quad (0\text{--}100)
  \label{eq:personal} \\
  \text{workBurnoutAvg} &= \frac{1}{7}\sum_{i=7}^{13} v_i
    \quad (0\text{--}100)
  \label{eq:work} \\
  \text{mentalFatigueScore} &=
    \frac{\text{personalBurnoutAvg} + \text{workBurnoutAvg}}{20}
    \quad (0.0\text{--}10.0)
  \label{eq:fatigue}
\end{align}

\noindent where $v_i$ is the adjusted answer value for question~$i$
(with reverse scoring applied to question~10).

\subsubsection{Alert Rules}

Alert rule evaluation is executed within the same MySQL transaction that writes the assessment result:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Threshold alert:} If the predicted burn rate is classified as \texttt{high} or \texttt{critical}, a corresponding \texttt{high\_risk} or \texttt{critical\_risk} alert is inserted.

  \item \textbf{Trend spike alert:} The service retrieves the most recent prior assessment for the same user. If the new predicted burn rate exceeds the prior value by 0.15 or more, a \texttt{trend\_spike} alert is generated.
\end{enumerate}

Both rules may trigger on the same assessment, producing up to two alert records per submission.

\subsection{AI Microservice Design}

\subsubsection{Dataset Description}

The model was trained on the ``Are Your Employees Burning Out?'' dataset from Kaggle~\cite{kaggle2021}, containing approximately 22{,}000 employee records. Although the dataset includes nine columns, only three features and one target variable were used:

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \singlespacing
  \caption{Dataset columns used for model training.}
  \label{tab:dataset-columns}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Column} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Range} & \textbf{Role} \\
    \midrule
    Designation          & Integer & 1--5    & Feature \\
    Resource Allocation  & Integer & 1--10   & Feature (has nulls) \\
    Mental Fatigue Score & Float   & 0.0--10.0 & Feature (has nulls) \\
    Burn Rate            & Float   & 0.0--1.0  & Target variable \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The remaining columns (Employee ID, Date of Joining, Gender, Company Type, WFH Setup Available) were excluded because they are not available in the live system. The three selected features correspond exactly to what the application can supply at inference time: designation and resource allocation from the simulated HR profile, and mental fatigue score from the CBI assessment.

\subsubsection{Feature Selection Justification}

Restricting the feature set to three variables serves three purposes. First, it aligns the model with the data the application actually collects, avoiding the need to impute or fabricate features that would require a real factory integration. Second, a three-feature model is straightforward to interpret and explain, which is important both for academic evaluation and for building end-user trust in the predictions. Third, because the simulated HR values are drawn from the same ranges as the training data, the model consistently receives in-distribution inputs, reducing the risk of extrapolation. A limitation of this approach is that the excluded columns, particularly Gender, Company Type, and WFH Setup, may carry some predictive signal that is not captured by the current feature set.

\subsubsection{Data Preprocessing}

Preprocessing involved three steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Target filtering:} Rows with a missing Burn Rate value were dropped, as they cannot contribute to supervised learning.

  \item \textbf{Missing value imputation:} Null values in Resource Allocation and Mental Fatigue Score were filled with the column median, which is more robust to skewed distributions than the mean.

  \item \textbf{Train/test split:} The dataset was divided into 80\% training and 20\% test sets with \texttt{random\_state=42} for reproducibility.
\end{enumerate}

At inference time, the Node.js backend defaults a missing \texttt{resource\_allocation} to 5 before forwarding the request to the Python service, ensuring the model always receives a complete input vector.

\subsubsection{Model Selection}

Two models were trained for comparison:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Linear Regression (baseline):} Establishes a performance floor and provides directly interpretable coefficients.

  \item \textbf{Random Forest Regressor (primary):} Selected for its robustness to non-linear relationships, tolerance of outliers, invariance to feature scale, and built-in feature importance scores.
\end{itemize}

Other ensemble methods were considered but not pursued. Gradient Boosting variants (XGBoost, LightGBM) offer marginal accuracy improvements over Random Forest on low-dimensional tabular data while introducing substantially more hyperparameter complexity. Neural networks require more extensive tuning to outperform tree ensembles on this type of data and are harder to interpret. SVR requires careful feature scaling and does not expose feature importance. k-NN degrades with missing values and scales poorly with dataset size. Given the three-feature input and the requirement for interpretable importance scores, Random Forest was the most appropriate choice.

\subsubsection{FastAPI Service Architecture}

The AI microservice (writed in Python) is a thin HTTP wrapper around the serialized
model:

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{06-ai-service-arch.png}
  \caption{Architecture of the FastAPI(Python) AI microservice.}
  \label{fig:ai-service-arch}
\end{figure}

The model file (\texttt{burnout\_model.pkl}) is loaded once at startup through a FastAPI lifespan handler and stored in a module-level variable. \subsubsection{Data Flow Pattern}

Server communication is organized around a consistent four-layer pattern:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Page component}: the UI is rendered and a TanStack Query hook is consumed.
  \item \textbf{Custom hook} (\texttt{useXxx}): \texttt{useQuery} or \texttt{useMutation} is wrapped, with cache keys, enabled conditions, and invalidation rules defined.
  \item \textbf{API function} (\texttt{lib/api/}): a typed \texttt{apiFetch<T>()} call is made with the endpoint path and parameters.
  \item \textbf{Fetch client} (\texttt{lib/api/client.ts}): the base URL is prepended, the JWT from \texttt{localStorage} is injected, the \texttt{\{success, data\}} envelope is unwrapped, and a typed \texttt{ApiError} is thrown on failure. On 401, the token is cleared and a redirect to login is triggered.
\end{enumerate}

\texttt{useMutation} is used for mutations, and cache invalidation on success keeps the UI automatically fresh without full page reloads.

\subsection{Backend Implementation}

\subsubsection{Project Structure and Configuration}

The backend is organized according to the folder structure described in
Section~3.3.1. A clear separation is maintained between configuration,
middleware, models, services, controllers, routes, validators, and
utilities. At startup, all environment variables are validated by a Zod
schema in \texttt{config/env.js}. If any required variable is missing or
malformed, the process exits immediately with a descriptive error message.
Because of this, the server is never started in a half-configured state.

During startup, database connections are established in sequence. First,
MySQL is pinged by \texttt{server.js}, then a connection to MongoDB is made,
and finally the HTTP listener is started. If any step fails, the process is
aborted. Graceful shutdown is handled through SIGINT and SIGTERM handlers.
The HTTP server is closed first, followed by both database connections.

\subsubsection{Authentication Flow Implementation}

A user record and a synthetic HR profile are created within a single MySQL
transaction during registration:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The request body is validated with Zod (email, password, name,
    gender, date of birth).
  \item The password is hashed with bcrypt, using configurable salt rounds.
  \item The user row is inserted into the \texttt{users} table.
  \item A simulated HR profile is generated: a random department is
    assigned, and designation (1--5), resource allocation (1--10), shift type,
    company type, WFH availability, and date of joining are generated.
  \item The HR profile is inserted into \texttt{hr\_profiles}.
  \item A JWT containing the user's ID and role is signed, and the token
    and user object are returned.
\end{enumerate}

The transaction wrapper makes sure that a failure during HR profile
generation does not leave an orphaned user record. During login,
credentials are validated against the bcrypt hash, and a JWT is issued with
a configurable expiration (default 7~days).

\subsubsection{Quiz Scoring Implementation}

The full pipeline described in Section~3.3.3 is implemented by the quiz
submission handler in \texttt{quiz.service.js}. The key implementation
details are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Answer validation:} Exactly 13 answers must be submitted.
    All question IDs are checked for validity, duplicate IDs are rejected,
    and all answer values must belong to the allowed set
    \{0, 25, 50, 75, 100\}.

  \item \textbf{Reverse scoring:} Question~10 (``Do you have enough
    energy for family and friends during leisure time?'') is reverse-scored
    using \texttt{adjustedValue = 100 - rawValue}. Both the raw and adjusted
    values are recorded in the enriched response object.

  \item \textbf{MongoDB-first persistence:} The raw submission is saved
    to MongoDB before the AI call. This way, answers are never lost even
    if subsequent steps fail. On failure, the orphan document is cleaned up.

  \item \textbf{Transactional MySQL write:} The assessment result
    insertion and alert rule evaluation happen within a single MySQL
    transaction. The MongoDB document is then back-linked with the MySQL
    assessment ID.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Dashboard Aggregation}

Company-wide and per-department statistics are computed by the admin
dashboard endpoint in a single API call. The latest assessment per employee
is found using a correlated subquery that selects the most recent
\texttt{assessment\_results.id} for each user. This result set is then
aggregated in JavaScript to produce:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Company overview:} total employees, total assessments,
    average burn rate, and risk distribution counts.
  \item \textbf{Per-department breakdown:} employee count, average burn
    rate, high-risk count, and risk distribution.
  \item \textbf{Department KPIs:} average composite KPI per department
    (mean of attendance, productivity, and quality scores).
  \item \textbf{Recent alerts:} the 10 most recent alert records.
\end{itemize}

Sorting, filtering (by risk level, shift type, and designation), and
server-side pagination are added by the department drill-down endpoint.
Anonymous IDs (``Worker \#0001'') are used to display employees,
satisfying the privacy requirement.

\subsubsection{Alert System Implementation}

Two rules are evaluated by the alert service after every assessment, as
described in Section~3.3.4. Both the threshold check and the trend spike
comparison are executed within the same MySQL transaction that wrote the
assessment result. The trend spike threshold (default 0.15) can be
configured through the \texttt{ALERT\_TREND\_SPIKE\_DELTA} environment
variable.

\subsubsection{FastAPI Prediction Endpoint}

Strict ranges are enforced at the prediction endpoint by Pydantic models:
designation (1--5), resource allocation (1--10), and mental fatigue score
(0--10). The model is loaded once at startup through a lifespan handler
and stored in memory. For each prediction call, a feature array is
constructed, \texttt{model.predict()} is run, the result is clamped to
the [0,~1] range, and the burn rate is returned in the standard envelope
format. Response times are consistently under 10~milliseconds.

\subsubsection{Integration with Express Backend}

A dual-mode architecture is implemented in the Express backend's
\texttt{ai.service.js}, controlled by the \texttt{AI\_USE\_MOCK}
environment variable. When set to \texttt{false}, an HTTP POST is sent by
the service to the Python endpoint with the three features, and
\texttt{response.data.predictedBurnRate} is parsed. An
\texttt{AbortController} is used by the request with a configurable timeout
(\texttt{AI\_SERVICE\_TIMEOUT\_MS}); timeouts and non-2xx responses are
wrapped as \texttt{ApiError.internal()} and surfaced to the frontend as a
generic 500 error.

When set to \texttt{true}, a mock formula is applied in-process:
\begin{equation}
  \text{burnRate} = \text{clamp}_{[0,1]}\!\left(
    0.07 \cdot \text{mfs} + 0.03 \cdot d + 0.02 \cdot ra - 0.05
    + \text{noise}
  \right)
  \label{eq:mock-formula}
\end{equation}

\noindent where \textit{mfs} represents the mental fatigue score,
\textit{d} represents designation, \textit{ra} represents resource
allocation, and \textit{noise} is uniform random in
$[\!-0.025, +0.025]$. No code changes are needed to switch between
modes, only an environment variable update and a server restart.

\subsubsection{State Management with TanStack Query}

All server state is managed through TanStack Query (React Query), which
provides automatic caching, background refetching, and cache invalidation.
Custom hooks in \texttt{lib/hooks/} wrap \texttt{useQuery} for read
operations and \texttt{useMutation} for write operations. Related query
keys are automatically invalidated by mutations on success, so the UI
stays synchronized with the server without manual refetch calls.

Skeleton placeholders (pulsing sand-coloured blocks) are used for
loading states. Inline messages in crimson-tinted cards are shown for
error states. An \texttt{EmptyState} component; with an icon,
descriptive text, and a call-to-action button; is displayed for empty
data sets.

\section{Discussion and Evaluation}

\subsection{Model Performance Analysis}

The Random Forest model achieved an $R^{2}$ of 0.90, an MAE of 0.048, and an RMSE of 0.061 on the held-out test set (Table~\ref{tab:model-comparison}). The model explains 90\% of burn rate variance and its predictions are typically within 5 percentage points on the 0--1 scale. The improvement over Linear Regression of 3.6 percentage points in $R^{2}$ indicates that the relationship between the three features and burn rate contains non-linear structure that a linear model cannot capture without additional feature engineering.

Feature importance analysis reveals that the mental fatigue score contributes approximately 85\% of predictive weight. Resource allocation accounts for roughly 10\% and designation for approximately 5\%. This distribution is consistent with the hypothesis that an employee's psychological state, as measured by the CBI, is a stronger predictor of burnout risk than organisational role or workload assignment. It also validates the system design, where the CBI quiz is the primary data collection mechanism.

The residual analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:residual-analysis}) shows prediction errors distributed randomly around zero with no systematic bias across the burn rate range, suggesting the model does not consistently over- or under-predict at any particular risk level. Residuals are approximately normally distributed and centred at zero.

Compared to public Kaggle notebooks trained on the same dataset using all available features, the three-feature model's $R^{2}$ of 0.90 falls within the reported range of 0.90--0.93 for Random Forest models. This suggests that most of the predictive signal in the dataset is concentrated in the three selected features, and the additional columns contribute relatively little incremental information. However, this observation is specific to this dataset and may not generalise to other organisational contexts where variables such as gender or remote work availability play a larger role.

\subsection{Answering the Research Questions}

\textbf{RQ1: How effectively can a Random Forest regression model predict
employee burn rate using only three input features?}

An $R^{2}$ of 0.90 and an MAE of 0.048 are achieved by the Random
Forest model. This demonstrates that three features: designation,
resource allocation, and mental fatigue score are enough for effective
burn rate prediction. Published results using the full feature set are
comparable to the model's performance, which indicates that the dominant
signal in the data is captured by the three selected features.

\textbf{RQ2: Can a web-based system provide actionable burnout insights to
both employees and HR administrators?}

The employee portal provides personalised burn rate gauges, historical
trend charts, section score breakdowns, and contextual help resources,
enabling workers to monitor their own mental health proactively. The admin
portal provides department heatmaps, risk distribution charts,
KPI-vs-burn-rate correlations, and automated alerts, giving HR managers
actionable visibility into organisational burnout patterns. The functional
testing results confirm that all data flows correctly from quiz submission
through prediction to dashboard visualisation.

\textbf{RQ3: What are the practical benefits and trade-offs of using a
microservice architecture to separate ML inference from the application
backend?}

The microservice architecture provides three concrete benefits:
(1)~technology heterogeneity, Python's scikit-learn ecosystem for ML and
JavaScript's Express ecosystem for web logic are each used where they excel;
(2)~independent development, the AI service can be retrained and redeployed
without modifying or restarting the backend; (3)~the mock/real switch
(\texttt{AI\_USE\_MOCK}) enables fully functional development without running
the Python service. The primary trade-off is increased operational
complexity: two servers must be running and communicating, and network
failures between them require timeout handling and error propagation.

\subsection{Comparison with Related Work}

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:related-comparison}, this system is the only
one among the reviewed works that brings together all four elements: a
trained ML model, a deployed web application, organisational-level
analytics, and a validated burnout instrument (CBI).

Pradhan and Jena's framework~\cite{pradhan2019} applies machine learning
for burnout classification, but it does not include a web deployment or
organisational dashboards. Commercial platforms like Culture
Amp~\cite{cultureamp2024} provide web-based analytics, yet they depend on
general-purpose engagement surveys rather than ML prediction. Kaggle
community notebooks~\cite{kaggle2021} show model training on the same
dataset, but predictions are not integrated into a usable application.

This thesis addresses the integration gap by combining psychometric
assessment, machine learning prediction, and multi-level analytics into a
single platform that serves both individual workers and organisational
decision makers.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

\subsection{Conclusion}

This thesis presented the design, implementation, and evaluation of an
intelligent web-based framework for the early detection of employee burnout
in industrial workplaces. The system brings together a validated
psychometric instrument (the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory), a machine
learning prediction model (Random Forest regression), and a full-stack
web application with role-based portals for both employees and
administrators.

The Random Forest model, trained on approximately 22{,}000 records from a
public dataset, achieves an $R^{2}$ of 0.90 using only three input
features. This demonstrates that effective burn rate prediction is
possible even with a minimal feature set. The employee portal equips
workers with self-monitoring tools, including burn rate gauges, trend
charts, and contextual support resources. Meanwhile, the admin portal
offers organisational-level visibility through department heatmaps, risk
distribution analytics, KPI correlations, and automated alerts.

The microservice architecture successfully separates the machine learning
concern (Python/FastAPI) from the web application backend
(Node.js/Express), which allows independent development and deployment.
End-to-end functional testing confirmed that all core workflows operate
correctly, from quiz submission and scoring through AI prediction to
dashboard visualisation and alert generation.

\subsection{Contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An integrated burnout detection platform} that combines
    psychometric assessment, machine learning prediction, and
    multi-level organisational analytics within a single web-based
    system. This combination is not found in any of the reviewed related
    works.

  \item \textbf{A practical CBI-to-ML pipeline} that transforms
    13-question Copenhagen Burnout Inventory responses into a continuous
    burn rate prediction through a clearly documented scoring and
    feature engineering process. This includes reverse scoring, section
    averaging, and normalisation.

  \item \textbf{A demonstrable microservice pattern} for integrating

    switch mechanism that enables independent development of the ML and
    web components.

  \item \textbf{A comprehensive admin analytics suite} including
    department heatmaps, risk distribution charts, KPI-vs-burn-rate
    correlations, and automated three-type alert generation
    (high risk, critical risk, and trend spike).

  \item \textbf{A complete design system and component library} with
    15 reusable UI primitives and 12 data visualisation components,
    including a custom SVG burn rate gauge.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations should be acknowledged:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulated HR data.} Designation and resource allocation
    values are randomly generated at registration rather than sourced
    from a real factory HR system. While values are drawn from the
    training dataset's ranges, they do not reflect actual workplace
    conditions.

  \item \textbf{Limited feature set.} The model uses only three
    features. Predictive accuracy could be improved by including
    additional signals such as actual shift patterns, overtime records,
    or longitudinal CBI trends.

  \item \textbf{Single dataset.} Model training and evaluation rely on a
    single public Kaggle dataset. The model's performance may not
    generalise across different industries, cultures, or organisational
    contexts.

  \item \textbf{Simulated KPI data.} The employee KPI records
    (attendance, productivity, quality) are seeded rather than collected
    from real workplace systems, which limits the validity of the
    KPI-vs-burn-rate correlation analysis.

  \item \textbf{Desktop-only interface.} The application is designed for
    minimum 992~px screen widths. Factory workers using personal mobile
    devices cannot access the system.

  \item \textbf{No formal usability study.} The system was tested
    functionally but not evaluated through a structured usability study
    with real end users.

  \item \textbf{No real-time updates.} The admin dashboard uses
    polling-based data fetching via TanStack Query rather than real-time
    push notifications via WebSocket or Server-Sent Events.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{subsec:future}

The following directions are proposed for future development:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mobile responsive design.} Extend the frontend with
    responsive breakpoints to support mobile and tablet access, enabling
    factory workers to complete assessments on personal devices.

  \item \textbf{Real-time alerts.} Implement WebSocket or Server-Sent
    Events to push new alerts to the admin dashboard instantly, without
    requiring page refresh or polling intervals.

  \item \textbf{Integration with real HR systems.} Replace the simulated
    HR profile generator with connectors to actual HR information
    systems (e.g., SAP SuccessFactors, BambooHR) to provide authentic
    designation and workload data.

  \item \textbf{Advanced ML models.} Explore Gradient Boosting
    (XGBoost, LightGBM) and time-series models that incorporate
    longitudinal CBI trends, enabling the system to predict burn rate
    trajectories rather than point estimates.

  \item \textbf{Expanded feature engineering.} Incorporate additional
    features such as actual overtime hours, shift rotation frequency,
    and seasonal patterns to improve model accuracy.

  \item \textbf{Export functionality.} Add CSV and PDF export for
    department analytics reports and assessment history, enabling
    offline review and reporting.

  \item \textbf{Accessibility improvements.} Add ARIA labels to custom
    components (gauge, heatmap) and conduct automated accessibility
    testing to ensure compliance with WCAG guidelines.

  \item \textbf{Multi-language support.} Internationalise the user
    interface to support Vietnamese and other languages relevant to the
    target industrial workforce.

  \item \textbf{Formal usability evaluation.} Conduct a structured
    usability study with factory workers and HR personnel to validate
    the system's effectiveness and identify user experience
    improvements.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Appendix C: Copenhagen Burnout Inventory Questionnaire}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Appendix C: CBI Questionnaire}

The system uses 13 questions from the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory,
covering two sections. Response options for all questions are: Always
(100), Often (75), Sometimes (50), Seldom (25), Never/almost never (0).

\subsection*{C.1 Personal Burnout (Questions 1--6)}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item How often do you feel tired?
  \item How often are you physically exhausted?
  \item How often are you emotionally exhausted?
  \item How often do you think: ``I can't take it anymore''?
  \item How often do you feel worn out?
  \item How often do you feel weak and susceptible to illness?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{C.2 Work-Related Burnout (Questions 7--13)}

\begin{enumerate}
  \setcounter{enumi}{6}
  \item Do you feel worn out at the end of the working day?
  \item Are you exhausted in the morning at the thought of another day
    at work?
  \item Do you feel that every working hour is tiring for you?
  \item Do you have enough energy for family and friends during leisure
    time? \textit{(Reverse-scored: adjustedValue = 100 $-$ rawValue)}
  \item Is your work emotionally exhausting?
  \item Does your work frustrate you?
  \item Do you feel burnt out because of your work?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{C.3 Scoring}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Calculate the personal burnout average: mean of questions 1--6
    (range 0--100).
  \item Calculate the work-related burnout average: mean of questions
    7--13 (range 0--100), with question~10 reverse-scored.
  \item Calculate the mental fatigue score:
    $\text{mentalFatigueScore} = \frac{\text{personalAvg} +
    \text{workAvg}}{20}$ (range 0.0--10.0).
\end{enumerate}
