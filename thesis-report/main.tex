\begin{center}
  \textbf{ABSTRACT}
\end{center}

\vspace{1cm}

Occupational burnout is becoming a serious problem in industrial workplaces. Workers there often deal with heavy workloads, repetitive tasks, and shift-based schedules that keep putting psychological pressure on them day after day. The usual way to detect burnout is through periodic surveys or manager observation, but these methods are reactive rather than preventive. By the time the symptoms show up, productivity has already dropped and health problems may have already started.

This thesis presents the design and implementation of an intelligent web-based framework for early detection of employee burnout in industrial settings. The system uses a validated psychometric tool, the \textbf{Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI)}, combined with a \textbf{Random Forest} regression model to predict a continuous burnout score from three features: employee designation, resource allocation, and a mental fatigue score derived from the CBI quiz responses.

The framework is built on a microservice architecture with three main components. A \textbf{React/Next.js} frontend provides two role-based portals: one for employees to do self-assessment, track their trends, and access support resources, and one for admins with department-level heatmaps, risk distribution charts, KPI analytics, and an automated alert inbox. A \textbf{Node.js/Express} backend handles authentication, quiz scoring, risk classification, alert generation, and dashboard aggregation, using \textbf{MySQL} for relational data and \textbf{MongoDB} for the quiz bank and raw submissions. A \textbf{Python/FastAPI} microservice loads the trained model at startup and serves predictions through a RESTful endpoint, keeping the machine learning part separate from the main application backend.

The Random Forest model was trained on around 22{,}000 records from a public Kaggle dataset. It achieves a coefficient of determination ($R^{2}$) of about 0.90, a mean absolute error (MAE) of about 0.048, and a root mean squared error (RMSE) of about 0.061 on the held-out test set, beating the Linear Regression baseline across all three metrics. Feature importance analysis shows that the mental fatigue score is the strongest predictor, making up nearly 81\% of predictive power of the model.

There are still some limitations. The HR profile data for designation and resource allocation is simulated (auto-generated), not taken from a real factory system. The model only uses three features, and the evaluation is based on a single public dataset that may not generalize to all industrial domains. No formal usability study was conducted either. In spite of these constraints, the system shows a practical architecture for combining psychometric assessment, machine learning prediction, and organizational analytics into one unified platform for proactive burnout management.

\bigskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} framework, employee's mental health, employee burnout detection, Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, Random Forest regression, Linear regression, microservice architecture, industrial workforce, web-based health monitoring.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}

The industrial workforce works under conditions that make occupational burnout very likely. Factory employees regularly deal with physically demanding tasks, strict shift schedules, production quotas, and very little control over their working conditions. The World Health Organization recognised burnout as an occupational phenomenon in the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), defining it as a syndrome caused by chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed~\cite{who2019}. The three main signs of burnout: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment; show up in measurable ways: decreased productivity, more absence, higher turnover rates, and increased healthcare costs~\cite{maslach2001}.

Global estimates say that stress-related conditions, including burnout, cost companies and corporations hundreds of billions of dollars every year through lost productivity and medical expenses~\cite{gallup2024}. In manufacturing and industrial sectors, the consequences go beyond individual well-being: tired workers are more likely to cause safety incidents, quality defects, and equipment damage. Despite these risks, most organizations still depend on ad-hoc observation and infrequent surveys to check employee mental health, an approach that is reactive rather than preventive.

Advances in machine learning (ML) and web technologies have opened up a chance to move burnout management from reactive response to proactive detection. By combining validated psychometric instruments with predictive models and real-time dashboards, it is now possible to build systems that continuously assess burnout risk at both the individual and organisational level. These systems can alert management to emerging problems before they become worse, while also giving employees self-monitoring tools and timely access to support resources.

\subsection{Motivating Scenario and Use Cases}

Consider a mid-sized manufacturing plant with 200 workers across 6 departments, running rotating day and night shifts. The HR team there has only three staff members, and they have to handle attendance, grievances, and benefits all at once. Right now, if they want to spot a worker at risk of burnout, they have to wait for a supervisor to notice visible signs like chronic fatigue, irritability, or decreased output, then report it to HR. By the time that happens, the worker may have already gone through weeks or months of worsening mental health, and the department may have already suffered measurable productivity losses.

This thesis proposes a system that turns this reactive workflow into a proactive one. Two main use cases drive the design:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Employee self-assessment and trend tracking.} Each worker periodically fills out a short 13-question burnout quiz on a web-based portal. The system right away calculates a predicted burn rate using a machine learning model and returns the result through an easy-to-read gauge visualization. Historical trends are displayed as line charts, so the worker can see how their burnout risk changes over time. If the predicted risk is high or critical, the system brings up curated mental health resources directly on the results page.

  \item \textbf{HR department-level monitoring and alerts.} The admin portal gathers assessment data across the whole workforce. A department heatmap shows average burn rates by organizational unit, letting HR quickly see which departments are under the most strain. Automated alerts notify HR when a worker's burn rate crosses a critical threshold or jumps sharply between consecutive assessments. Drill-down views reveal anonymized employee-level detail, keeping privacy intact while still enabling targeted intervention.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Even though it is well-known that burnout negatively affect industrial productivity and worker welfare, there are still several gaps in how companies handle it:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Detection is reactive.} Most organizations only spot burnout after its effects such as absenteeism, errors, resignations, have already shown up. There is no systematic way to get an early warning.

  \item \textbf{Psychometric data is underutilized.} Validated burnout tools like the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI) exist and are freely available, yet they are rarely built into continuous monitoring workflows. When surveys are done, the results are usually analyzed manually and in isolation.

  \item \textbf{No predictive capability.} Traditional approaches only report current symptoms but do not predict future risk. Machine learning can fill this gap by learning patterns from historical data and estimating burn rates from just a few measurable inputs.

  \item \textbf{No organizational-level visibility.} Individual survey results stay siloed. HR managers do not have aggregated, visual tools such as department heatmaps, risk distribution charts, and trend analyses that would let them make data-driven workforce management decisions.
\end{enumerate}

This thesis tackles these gaps by designing and implementing an integrated web-based framework that brings together psychometric assessment, machine learning prediction, and multi-level analytics in a single platform.

\subsection{Scope and Objectives}

The scope of this thesis covers the design, implementation, and evaluation of a desktop web application for early burnout detection in industrial workplaces. The system uses simulated HR data (since real factory records are private and inaccessible), the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory as the psychometric tool, and a Random Forest regression model trained on a public dataset as the predictive engine.

The specific objectives are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Implement a web-based Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI) assessment that turns employee responses into a mental fatigue score ready for machine learning input.

  \item Train and evaluate a Random Forest regression model to predict employee burn rate from three features: designation, resource allocation, and mental fatigue score.

  \item Build an employee self-monitoring portal with a burn rate gauge, historical trend charts, section score breakdowns, and contextual access to mental health resources.

  \item Build an administrative command center with department heatmaps, risk distribution analytics, KPI correlation views, and a drill-down capability for individual employee analysis.

  \item Implement an automated alert system that notifies administrators when employees cross critical risk thresholds or show sharp burn rate increases between assessments.

  \item Demonstrate a microservice architecture that cleanly separates the machine learning inference part (Python/FastAPI) from the application backend (Node.js/Express) and the user interface (React/Next.js).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research Questions}

This thesis investigates the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1.}] How effectively can a Random Forest regression
    model predict employee burn rate using only three input features
    (designation, resource allocation, and mental fatigue score)?

  \item[\textbf{RQ2.}] Can a web-based system provide actionable burnout
    insights to both employees (through self-monitoring tools) and HR
    administrators (through department-level analytics and automated
    alerts)?

  \item[\textbf{RQ3.}] What are the practical benefits and trade-offs of
    using a microservice architecture to separate machine learning
    inference from the application backend in a health monitoring
    system?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Assumptions and Proposed Solution}

The following assumptions underlie the design of the system:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulated HR data.} Real factory HR databases are
    private and inaccessible for academic research. The backend
    generates synthetic HR profiles (designation, resource allocation,
    department, shift type) at registration time, drawing values from
    the same ranges as the training dataset to ensure the ML model
    receives in-distribution inputs.

  \item \textbf{CBI as a valid burnout measure.} The Copenhagen Burnout
    Inventory is an established, freely available instrument with
    demonstrated reliability and validity in occupational health
    research. Its 13-question excerpt (personal and work-related
    burnout sections) is used as the sole actively collected data
    point.

  \item \textbf{Desktop-only usage.} The system targets HR office
    terminals and factory kiosk stations. No mobile-responsive layout
    is implemented.

  \item \textbf{Single-language interface.} The user interface is
    presented in English. Multi-language support is deferred to future
    work.
\end{itemize}

The proposed solution is a three-tier web app:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \textbf{React/Next.js frontend} with two role-based portals (employee and admin), connected to the backend through RESTful API calls, with TanStack Query handling server state.

  \item A \textbf{Node.js/Express backend} that manages authentication (JWT), quiz scoring (CBI with reverse scoring), risk classification, alert generation, dashboard aggregation, and resource management, using MySQL and MongoDB as databases.

  \item A \textbf{Python/FastAPI AI microservice} that loads a pre-trained Random Forest model at startup and exposes a single prediction endpoint. The backend can switch between the real AI service and an in-process mock formula through an environment variable, so each component can be developed and deployed independently.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Occupational Burnout}

\subsubsection{Definition and Impact on the Industrial Workforce}

The concept of occupational burnout was first introduced by Freudenberger
in 1974 to describe the state of physical and emotional depletion observed
among human service workers~\cite{freudenberger1974}. The construct was
later formalised by Maslach and Jackson, who defined burnout as a
three-dimensional syndrome comprising emotional exhaustion,
depersonalisation (cynicism), and reduced personal
accomplishment~\cite{maslach1981}. In 2019 the World Health Organization
included burnout in the ICD-11 as an occupational phenomenon, describing it
as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been
successfully managed~\cite{who2019}.

In industrial and manufacturing environments, the consequences of burnout
extend beyond individual suffering. Research has linked burnout to
increased absenteeism, higher employee turnover, reduced product quality,
and elevated workplace accident rates~\cite{ahola2017}. A Gallup
meta-analysis across industries found that employees experiencing burnout
are 63\% more likely to take a sick day, 2.6 times more likely to seek a
new job, and 13\% less confident in their performance~\cite{gallup2024}.
For factory settings with tight production schedules and safety-critical processes, these numbers translate directly into operational and financial risk.

\subsubsection{Burnout Measurement Instruments}

The two most widely used instruments for measuring occupational burnout are the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) and the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI).

The \textbf{Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI)}, developed in 1981~\cite{maslach1981}, is the most cited burnout measure in the literature. It has 22 items across three subscales (emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment). However, the MBI is copyrighted and requires a commercial licence, which limits its adoption in academic and open-source projects.

The \textbf{Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI)}, developed by Kristensen et~al.\ in 2005~\cite{kristensen2005}, was created as a freely available alternative. The CBI sees burnout as a state of physical and psychological fatigue and exhaustion, measured along three dimensions: personal burnout, work-related burnout, and client-related burnout. Each dimension is assessed through a set of questions scored on a five-point frequency scale ranging from ``Always'' (100 points) to ``Never/almost never'' (0 points). The CBI has shown high internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha exceeding 0.85 across all subscales) and strong predictive validity for outcomes such as sickness absence and sleep disturbance~\cite{kristensen2005}.

\subsubsection{The Copenhagen Burnout Inventory in This Thesis}

In this thesis, I use the CBI for some reasons. First, the CBI is
completely free to use, with no licensing restrictions, making it ideal for
an academic project. Second, the CBI distinguishes between personal and
work-related burnout, which allows the system to provide more granular
feedback to both employees and administrators. Third, the CBI's
straightforward frequency-based response scale maps naturally to a numeric
score that can be fed into a machine learning model.

Since the target population consists of industrial workers rather than
client-facing professionals, the client-related burnout subscale is omitted.
The system uses the remaining 13 questions: six for personal burnout and
seven for work-related burnout. One question (``Do you have enough energy
for family and friends during leisure time?'') is reverse-scored, as a
positive response indicates lower burnout. The section averages are combined
and normalised to a 0--10 scale to produce the mental fatigue score used as
input to the machine learning model.

\subsection{Machine Learning for Health Prediction}

\subsubsection{Supervised Regression}

Machine learning tasks are broadly categorised into supervised and
unsupervised learning. In supervised learning, the algorithm is trained on
a labelled dataset where each input is associated with a known
output~\cite{bishop2006}. When the output is a continuous numeric value,
the task is called regression.

Burnout prediction fits naturally into the supervised regression paradigm:
the training dataset contains employee observations with a known burn rate
(the target variable), and the goal is to learn a function that maps
employee features to a predicted burn rate for new, unseen employees.

\subsubsection{Linear Regression}

Linear Regression is the most straightforward regression algorithm we can use. It essentially treats the connection between our inputs and our target as a basic straight-line equation:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{y} = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + w_3 x_3 + b
  \label{eq:linear-regression}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $x_1, x_2,$ and $x_3$ represent the inputs that we are looking at (designation, resource allocation, and mental fatigue score). The terms $w_1, w_2,$ and $w_3$ are the weights the model learns over time, and $b$ is a starting bias. The model figures out the best weights by trying to make the squared differences between its guesses and the real answers as small as possible~\cite{hastie2009}.

I am using Linear Regression as a starting baseline for this thesis because its simplicity gives us a clear performance floor. We can use this basic standard to see whether our more complex models are actually doing any better. Also, its learned weights are really easy to read directly: if we see a big weight on the mental fatigue score, for example, it tells us right away that fatigue is the main thing driving the burn rate.

\subsubsection{Random Forest Regression}

Random Forest is a group-based learning method originally created by Breiman~\cite{breiman2001}. Instead of building just one model, it creates a whole collection of decision trees. Each tree learns from a random, mixed-up chunk of the training data and looks at a random handful of features whenever it needs to make a decision split. For the final prediction, it just averages together what all the individual trees guessed.

Random Forest offers some great advantages over Linear Regression for this specific problem:

\begin{itemize}
  \item It can figure out complicated, non-linear relationships and how features naturally interact with each other, meaning I don't have to manually engineer new features.
  \item It doesn't get messed up by extreme outliers as easily, because the individual trees make decisions at specific thresholds instead of forcing a single, global line through all the points.
  \item It is perfectly fine with features being on completely different scales. This means that variables like designation (which goes from 1 to 5) and resource allocation (which goes from 1 to 10) do not need to be normalized beforehand.
  \item It automatically provides built-in feature importance scores, which makes it easy to see exactly what is helping the model make its predictions.
\end{itemize}

When working with spreadsheet-style datasets that have a handful of columns and tens of thousands of rows, Random Forest generally does a great job without needing a massive amount of fine-tuning. This makes it a really practical and reliable choice for a thesis-sized project~\cite{fernandez2014}.

\subsubsection{Model Evaluation Metrics}

To figure out how well these regression models are actually performing, I am relying on three complementary metrics:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE):} This is simply the average of how far off our predictions are from the true values. For instance, on our 0--1 burn rate scale, an MAE of 0.05 means the model's guess is typically wrong by about 5 percentage points.

  \item \textbf{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):} This metric is the square root of our averaged squared mistakes. Because of how the math works out, RMSE punishes the model a lot more for making huge errors. This helps us easily spot if the model is occasionally making some really bad outlier predictions.

  \item \textbf{Coefficient of Determination ($R^{2}$):} This metric tells us what proportion of the changes in our target variable can actually be explained by our model. An $R^{2}$ of 0.90 means our model understands and captures 90\% of why the burn rates are changing. A value closer to 1.0 means we have a much better fit.
\end{itemize}

Looking at all three of these scores together gives us a really complete and honest picture of model performance. MAE shows our everyday, average-case accuracy, RMSE checks on our worst-case sensitivity, and $R^{2}$ sums up the model's overall power to explain the data~\cite{hastie2009}.

\subsection{Web Application Architectures}

\subsubsection{Microservices vs.\ Monolithic Architecture}

A monolithic architecture basically bundles the entire application; such as the user interface, the business rules, the data access, and any machine learning features; into one massive, single package. This way of building things is really easy to set up at first, but it gets pretty difficult to maintain and expand when the application becomes larger. This is especially true when different parts of your app need totally different coding tools to run smoothly~\cite{newman2015}.

On the other hand, a microservice architecture breaks the application down into small, independent pieces that you can run on their own. Each of these smaller pieces, or services, focuses on doing just only one job really well~\cite{newman2015}. These services talk to each other over the internet using simple, lightweight formats like HTTP and REST. The best parts about doing it this way are that you can update one service without touching the rest, mix and match your programming languages (like using Python for your AI and JavaScript for your web pages), stop bugs from crashing the whole system, and allow different team members to work on separate things easily.

I decided to go with a microservice approach for this thesis because the machine learning part of the project has completely different things than the main web app. The AI component needs Python and a library called scikit-learn, but the web backend works best in the JavaScript ecosystem (using tools like Express and Mongoose). By pulling the AI service out and putting it into its own separate FastAPI microservice, my system stays neatly organized. This means I can build, test, and deploy the machine learning part totally independent of the website.

\subsubsection{RESTful API Design}

Representational State Transfer, or REST, is essentially a set of clear guidelines for building apps that communicate over a network~\cite{fielding2000}. RESTful APIs use standard web actions (GET, POST, PUT, PATCH, DELETE) to interact with data based on web URLs. The main ideas here include keeping everything ``stateless'' (which means every request carries all the details needed to figure it out, without the server needing to remember the past), keeping the interface predictable, and keeping the user-side and server-side completely separate.

In this project, both the connection between the frontend and the web backend, as well as the backend talking to the AI service, follow these standard REST rules. To make catching errors super simple on the user's side, all responses are wrapped up in a uniform JSON format, which always looks like either \texttt{\{success, data\}} or \texttt{\{success, error\}}.

\subsection{Related Technologies}

\subsubsection{React and Next.js for Frontend Development}

React is a popular JavaScript library that helps build user interfaces by snapping together self-contained, reusable blocks called components~\cite{react2024}. Each component can take in data (called props) and manage its own inner details using handy features known as hooks. The best thing about React is its ``virtual DOM''; instead of constantly reloading the entire webpage, it is smart enough to quickly update just the tiny pieces of the screen that actually changed. This makes the rendering process incredibly fast, even if you are displaying a large dashboard full of heavy data.

Next.js is essentially an upgraded frame that wraps around React. It adds a bunch of really helpful tools on top, like setting up web routes based directly on how your folders are structured, pre-building pages on the server to save time, and handling built-in APIs~\cite{nextjs2024}. I am using Next.js with its App Router feature for my thesis, which just turns my folder structure into links automatically, alongside Turbopack so my coding tests load super quickly. For managing all the data moving between the server and my screens, I use TanStack Query (React Query). It quietly handles the tough stuff behind the scenes: saving data so we don't ask for it twice, fetching fresh updates in the background, and clearing out outdated information when changes happen.

\subsubsection{Node.js and Express for Backend Services}

Node.js is a runtime system built directly onto Google Chrome's engine, which basically allows developers to run JavaScript on backend servers rather than just in internet browsers~\cite{nodejs2024}. It operates in an event-driven way and doesn't get blocked up waiting for tasks to finish, which makes it absolutely fantastic for juggling a ton of simultaneous API requests without lagging.

Express is a really clean, minimal framework for Node.js that gives you the basic tools to set up website paths, handle requests in steps, and deal with internet connections~\cite{express2024}. For my backend, I am using Express~5 and setting up the code in very clear, organized layers (breaking things into routes, controllers, services, and repositories) so no single file gets cluttered. I also use ``middleware'' stages to automatically handle tasks that apply to the whole app, like checking user logins (JWT), validating that incoming information looks correct (Zod schemas), and catching unexpected system crashes safely.

\subsubsection{FastAPI for Machine Learning Serving}

FastAPI is a modern web framework built in Python that is designed specifically to set up APIs really quickly, and it actually builds an interactive manual for itself as you code~\cite{fastapi2024}. It takes advantage of standard Python formatting and ``Pydantic'' models to strictly verify that any data sent its way is correct, and it is capable of handling tasks simultaneously without getting bogged down. The mix of running very fast, checking inputs for you, and drawing up its own documentation makes FastAPI an awesome choice for linking machine learning models to the web. In this project, the FastAPI service boots up, loads my scikit-learn model, and fires back prediction answers in under 10 milliseconds.

\section{Methodology and System Design}

\subsection{System Architecture Overview}

The system follows a three-part microservice setup to get everything working. It features a frontend built with React and Next.js, a web backend running on Node.js and Express, and a totally separate AI microservice powered by Python and FastAPI. Behind the scenes, the data is supported by both MySQL and MongoDB databases. All three parts of the system talk to each other over the web using standard RESTful HTTP APIs, and they all wrap their messages in the exact same JSON format to keep communication clear and simple.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{02-system-architecture.png}
  \caption{High-level system architecture.}
  \label{fig:system-architecture}
\end{figure}

I decided to split the project into three distinct services for a couple of reasons. First, the machine learning piece really needs Python and the scikit-learn library to run. At the same time, the main web backend is much easier to build using JavaScript, because its ecosystem is so good at handling web traffic, connecting to databases, and dealing with middleware tasks. Second, by keeping the AI service in its own little box, I can test it, work on it, and even restart it completely independently without crashing the main website. The backend is also set up with a handy switch (an environment variable). If I turn on \texttt{AI\_USE\_MOCK=true}, it just uses a simulated math formula for testing. If I flip it to \texttt{AI\_USE\_MOCK=false}, it actually sends requests to the real Python AI. This makes working on each part individually a lot less stressful.

\subsection{Database Design}

\subsubsection{MySQL Schema}

I use MySQL to hold all the highly structured, relational data. The design uses seven specific tables that are carefully linked together, so if a main record ever gets deleted, any connected records are automatically cleared out too.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{03-er-diagram.png}
  \caption{Entity-relationship diagram of the MySQL database.}
  \label{fig:er-diagram}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{users:} This table securely stores log-in information (keeping passwords safe with a bcrypt scramble), basic demographic details, and the user's role (either employee or admin).

  \item \textbf{departments:} This is simply a clean reference list of all the different factory departments (like Assembly Line~A, Maintenance, or Quality Control). It really helps organize data for the big admin heatmap.

  \item \textbf{hr\_profiles:} Every single user account has exactly one of these connected to it. It holds the pretend employee profile generated when they sign up: what department they are in, their designation level (1--5), their resource allocation score (1--10), whether they work a Day, Night, or Rotating shift, the company type, and if they can work from home. I made sure to map these numbers exactly to the ones used to train my AI model.

  \item \textbf{assessment\_results:} A new row gets dropped in here every time a user finishes a quiz. It keeps track of their personal burnout score (0--100), work burnout score (0--100), mental fatigue score (0--10), the predicted burn rate the AI guessed (0--1), and their general risk category. I added search indexes based on the user's ID and the date so the app can draw historical trend graphs very fast.

  \item \textbf{alerts:} These pop up automatically if a quiz reveals a dangerous stress level. The system supports three kinds: \texttt{high\_risk}, \texttt{critical\_risk}, and \texttt{trend\_spike}. I put an index on these so the website can instantly load an unread message inbox.

  \item \textbf{employee\_kpis:} This holds a monthly snapshot of how employees are doing on the actual job, things like their attendance, productivity scores, work quality, overtime, finished tasks, and sick days. The admin dashboard pulls this data to see if higher burnout is actually hurting factory performance.

  \item \textbf{help\_resources:} A list of curated mental health links, but each link has a set stress minimum. The system will only display a resource if its requirement matches or is below the employee's current stress level.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{MongoDB Collections}

I went with MongoDB for two collections simply because it works better with documents that do not need rigid, unbending structure:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{cbi\_questions:} This is just one large, single document holding the whole blueprint for the CBI quiz. It contains the quiz's title, its version, five different multiple-choice options with their math values, and it splits the quiz into two sections (personal and work-related). It also lists out all 13 questions, noting exactly which section they go in and whether they need to be scored backwards. Keeping this entire quiz in one single document means the system can just grab the whole thing at once, instead of trying to paste several database tables together.

  \item \textbf{quiz\_submissions:} For every completed test, one document gets placed in this collection. It keeps a record of the MySQL user's ID, what version of the quiz they took, and exactly how they answered all 13 questions (including calculated math corrections for tricky questions). It also pre-calculates their section averages and keeps a link pointing to their main score in the MySQL table. Storing it this way bundles a user's exact, detailed answers tightly together as one solid file, which is much cleaner than spreading them across a relational database.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Backend Design}

\subsubsection{Layered Architecture}

My Express backend relies on a strict four-layer design strategy. I did this to make absolute sure that the part answering web requests, the part doing heavy app math, and the part talking to the databases all stay entirely out of each other's way.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.80\linewidth]{04-backend-layer.png}
  \caption{Layered architecture of the Express backend.}
  \label{fig:backend-layers}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Routes:} These scripts map out the internet paths and attach special gateway steps. For example, they check if a person is actually logged in (\texttt{authenticate}), if they are allowed on the page (\texttt{requireRole}), and if the info they typed makes sense (checked against Zod rules). Routes basically never contain any real business rules.

  \item \textbf{Controllers:} Think of these as very basic middlemen. They pull user details out of the internet request, toss the tough work over to the "Services" layer, and then carefully wrap the Service's final answer up in the simple \texttt{\{success: true, data\}} envelope before handing it back to the internet.

  \item \textbf{Services:} This is where the heavy lifting and main logic happen. It is responsible for figuring out complex quiz scores, calculating the final fatigue number, bouncing questions to the AI, setting final risk groups, triggering danger alerts, crunching totals for the big dashboards, and safely organizing updates so database moves are secure.

  \item \textbf{Repositories:} These files have one job: safely talking to the databases. To talk to MySQL, they run parameterized SQL statements using a library called \texttt{mysql2/promise}. To connect to MongoDB, they use Mongoose tools. Literally no other piece of the app is ever allowed to poke the databases directly.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Authentication and Authorisation}

To control how users securely log in, the whole project utilizes JSON Web Tokens (JWT). When someone registers a new account, the app permanently scrambles their chosen password securely using bcrypt, then grants them a signed digital ticket (the JWT). That ticket holds just enough basic info, like their ID and role. Making the user account and creating the mock HR file happens in one combined database step, which keeps things extremely organized and prevents bugs.

For knowing who is allowed to view which pages, the router acts as a bouncer. The app has routes strictly looking for \texttt{role = ``employee''} and others just looking for \texttt{role = ``admin''}. Other general shared pages will allow access as long as someone is authenticated. Just to be completely safe against hackers trying to forge the login tokens, I added a rule in the settings forcing the digital server key to be a heavy secret string at least 16 characters long.

\subsubsection{Quiz Scoring Pipeline}

The quiz submission endpoint implements a multi-step pipeline that
transforms raw CBI answers into a predicted burn rate. The pipeline
operates as follows:

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{05-quiz-pipeline.png}
  \caption{Submitting Quiz results pipeline.}
  \label{fig:quiz-pipeline}
\end{figure}

The scoring formulae are:
\begin{align}
  \text{personalBurnoutAvg} &= \frac{1}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{6} v_i
    \quad (0\text{--}100)
  \label{eq:personal} \\
  \text{workBurnoutAvg} &= \frac{1}{7}\sum_{i=7}^{13} v_i
    \quad (0\text{--}100)
  \label{eq:work} \\
  \text{mentalFatigueScore} &=
    \frac{\text{personalBurnoutAvg} + \text{workBurnoutAvg}}{20}
    \quad (0.0\text{--}10.0)
  \label{eq:fatigue}
\end{align}

\noindent where $v_i$ is the adjusted answer value for question~$i$
(with reverse scoring applied to question~10).

\subsubsection{Alert Rules}

Right exactly when the database securely saves a completed quiz result in its main transaction, the app runs two separate alert checks behind the scenes:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Threshold alert:} If someone's burnout risk calculation comes back as ``high'' or ``critical'', the app immediately creates a \texttt{high\_risk} or \texttt{critical\_risk} warning notification.

  \item \textbf{Trend spike alert:} The system also looks back at how the person scored on their previous quiz. If their new predicted burn rate suddenly jumps up by 0.15 or more compared to last time, it sets off a \texttt{trend\_spike} alert to heavily flag that sudden increase.
\end{enumerate}

A single quiz attempt can absolutely trigger both of these rules at the exact same time, adding up to two separate alert messages into the database at once.

\subsection{AI Microservice Design}

\subsubsection{Dataset Description}

To actually teach the machine learning model how to predict burnout, I fed it a very popular public dataset from Kaggle named ``Are Your Employees Burning Out?''~\cite{kaggle2021}, which holds data points on around 22,000 distinct employees. Even though each row in this file technically contains nine different data columns, I narrowed things down a bit. I am only really using three specific columns as my main inputs, along with just one final column that serves as the ultimate target answer I want the AI to learn how to guess:

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \singlespacing
  \caption{Dataset columns used for model training.}
  \label{tab:dataset-columns}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Column} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Range} & \textbf{Role} \\
    \midrule
    Designation          & Integer & 1--5    & Feature \\
    Resource Allocation  & Integer & 1--10   & Feature (has nulls) \\
    Mental Fatigue Score & Float   & 0.0--10.0 & Feature (has nulls) \\
    Burn Rate            & Float   & 0.0--1.0  & Target variable \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

There are a few other columns in the dataset (like Employee ID, Date of Joining, Gender, Company Type, and WFH Setup Available), but I am not using them to train the model. I purposely kept the features limited to just three because, when the live app is actually running, these are the only three pieces of information we will realistically have: the designation and resource allocation come from the simulated HR profile, and the mental fatigue score comes directly from the user's CBI quiz.

\subsubsection{Feature Selection Justification}

Sticking to exactly three features helps the project in three main ways. First, it perfectly matches the model with the data the app actually collects, so I do not have to worry about pulling in fake data that would require a real factory connection. Second, using fewer features makes the model much easier to understand and explain. This transparency is super important for both thesis evaluation and making sure end-users actually trust the results. Finally, because the simulated HR numbers use the exact same ranges as the training data, the model always gets the kind of input it expects, which cuts down on weird or unreliable predictions.

\subsubsection{Data Preprocessing}

Getting the data cleaned up and ready took three main steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Dropping empty targets:} First, if the Burn Rate column is blank in any row, that row entirely is remove entirely. It is almost impossible to train the model without the final answer.

  \item \textbf{Filling in missing values:} If a Resource Allocation or Mental Fatigue Score is missing, I fill in the blank with the median of that column. I went with the median instead of the average because it handles weird outliers much better when we are dealing with a numbered rating scale.

  \item \textbf{Train/test split:} I split the data into 80\% for training and 20\% for testing. I also set a fixed random seed (\texttt{random\_state=42}) so that my results are reproducible and stay exactly the same every time the code is executed.
\end{enumerate}

When the app makes a prediction, the Node.js backend double-checks everything. If it sees a missing \texttt{resource\_allocation}, it automatically forces it to a default score of~5 (right in the middle) before sending it to the Python service. This makes sure the model always has all three numbers ready to go.

\subsubsection{Model Selection}

I decided to train and compare two different models:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Linear Regression (baseline):} I used this as a starting point. It gives me a basic standard for performance, and it is really easy to see exactly how the model weighs the different inputs.

  \item \textbf{Random Forest Regressor (primary):} This is my main model. I chose it because it easily handles complex, non-linear patterns, isn't easily thrown off by weird outliers, doesn't mind how the numbers are scaled, and naturally tells me which features actually mattered the most.
\end{itemize}

I definitely thought about using other methods (like Gradient Boosting, Neural Networks, SVR, and kNN), but decided against them. With only three features, Gradient Boosting models (like XGBoost or LightGBM) only give a tiny improvement but make setting up the model way more complicated. Neural networks need a lot more data and tweaking to beat simpler tree models on basic spreadsheet data. SVR gets too tricky with scaling the features, and kNN struggles with messy data while also failing to show which features were actually important.

\subsubsection{FastAPI Service Architecture}

The AI microservice (writed in Python) is a thin HTTP wrapper around the serialized
model:

\subsubsection{Data Flow Pattern}

Server communication is organized around a consistent four-layer pattern:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Page component}: the UI is rendered and a TanStack Query hook is consumed.
  \item \textbf{Custom hook} (\texttt{useXxx}): \texttt{useQuery} or \texttt{useMutation} is wrapped, with cache keys, enabled conditions, and invalidation rules defined.
  \item \textbf{API function} (\texttt{lib/api/}): a typed \texttt{apiFetch<T>()} call is made with the endpoint path and parameters.
  \item \textbf{Fetch client} (\texttt{lib/api/client.ts}): the base URL is prepended, the JWT from \texttt{localStorage} is injected, the \texttt{\{success, data\}} envelope is unwrapped, and a typed \texttt{ApiError} is thrown on failure. On 401, the token is cleared and a redirect to login is triggered.
\end{enumerate}

\texttt{useMutation} is used for mutations, and cache invalidation on success keeps the UI automatically fresh without full page reloads.

\subsection{Backend Implementation}

\subsubsection{Project Structure and Configuration}

The backend is organized according to the folder structure described in
Section~3.3.1. A clear separation is maintained between configuration,
middleware, models, services, controllers, routes, validators, and
utilities. At startup, all environment variables are validated by a Zod
schema in \texttt{config/env.js}. If any required variable is missing or
malformed, the process exits immediately with a descriptive error message.
Because of this, the server is never started in a half-configured state.

During startup, database connections are established in sequence. First,
MySQL is pinged by \texttt{server.js}, then a connection to MongoDB is made,
and finally the HTTP listener is started. If any step fails, the process is
aborted. Graceful shutdown is handled through SIGINT and SIGTERM handlers.
The HTTP server is closed first, followed by both database connections.

\subsubsection{Authentication Flow Implementation}

A user record and a synthetic HR profile are created within a single MySQL
transaction during registration:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The request body is validated with Zod (email, password, name,
    gender, date of birth).
  \item The password is hashed with bcrypt, using configurable salt rounds.
  \item The user row is inserted into the \texttt{users} table.
  \item A simulated HR profile is generated: a random department is
    assigned, and designation (1--5), resource allocation (1--10), shift type,
    company type, WFH availability, and date of joining are generated.
  \item The HR profile is inserted into \texttt{hr\_profiles}.
  \item A JWT containing the user's ID and role is signed, and the token
    and user object are returned.
\end{enumerate}

The transaction wrapper makes sure that a failure during HR profile
generation does not leave an orphaned user record. During login,
credentials are validated against the bcrypt hash, and a JWT is issued with
a configurable expiration (default 7~days).

\subsubsection{Quiz Scoring Implementation}

The full pipeline described in Section~3.3.3 is implemented by the quiz
submission handler in \texttt{quiz.service.js}. The key implementation
details are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Answer validation:} Exactly 13 answers must be submitted.
    All question IDs are checked for validity, duplicate IDs are rejected,
    and all answer values must belong to the allowed set
    \{0, 25, 50, 75, 100\}.

  \item \textbf{Reverse scoring:} Question~10 (``Do you have enough
    energy for family and friends during leisure time?'') is reverse-scored
    using \texttt{adjustedValue = 100 - rawValue}. Both the raw and adjusted
    values are recorded in the enriched response object.

  \item \textbf{MongoDB-first persistence:} The raw submission is saved
    to MongoDB before the AI call. This way, answers are never lost even
    if subsequent steps fail. On failure, the orphan document is cleaned up.

  \item \textbf{Transactional MySQL write:} The assessment result
    insertion and alert rule evaluation happen within a single MySQL
    transaction. The MongoDB document is then back-linked with the MySQL
    assessment ID.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Dashboard Aggregation}

Company-wide and per-department statistics are computed by the admin
dashboard endpoint in a single API call. The latest assessment per employee
is found using a correlated subquery that selects the most recent
\texttt{assessment\_results.id} for each user. This result set is then
aggregated in JavaScript to produce:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Company overview:} total employees, total assessments,
    average burn rate, and risk distribution counts.
  \item \textbf{Per-department breakdown:} employee count, average burn
    rate, high-risk count, and risk distribution.
  \item \textbf{Department KPIs:} average composite KPI per department
    (mean of attendance, productivity, and quality scores).
  \item \textbf{Recent alerts:} the 10 most recent alert records.
\end{itemize}

Sorting, filtering (by risk level, shift type, and designation), and
server-side pagination are added by the department drill-down endpoint.
Anonymous IDs (``Worker \#0001'') are used to display employees,
satisfying the privacy requirement.

\subsubsection{Alert System Implementation}

Two rules are evaluated by the alert service after every assessment, as
described in Section~3.3.4. Both the threshold check and the trend spike
comparison are executed within the same MySQL transaction that wrote the
assessment result. The trend spike threshold (default 0.15) can be
configured through the \texttt{ALERT\_TREND\_SPIKE\_DELTA} environment
variable.

\subsubsection{FastAPI Prediction Endpoint}

Strict ranges are enforced at the prediction endpoint by Pydantic models:
designation (1--5), resource allocation (1--10), and mental fatigue score
(0--10). The model is loaded once at startup through a lifespan handler
and stored in memory. For each prediction call, a feature array is
constructed, \texttt{model.predict()} is run, the result is clamped to
the [0,~1] range, and the burn rate is returned in the standard envelope
format. Response times are consistently under 10~milliseconds.

\subsubsection{Integration with Express Backend}

A dual-mode architecture is implemented in the Express backend's
\texttt{ai.service.js}, controlled by the \texttt{AI\_USE\_MOCK}
environment variable. When set to \texttt{false}, an HTTP POST is sent by
the service to the Python endpoint with the three features, and
\texttt{response.data.predictedBurnRate} is parsed. An
\texttt{AbortController} is used by the request with a configurable timeout
(\texttt{AI\_SERVICE\_TIMEOUT\_MS}); timeouts and non-2xx responses are
wrapped as \texttt{ApiError.internal()} and surfaced to the frontend as a
generic 500 error.

When set to \texttt{true}, a mock formula is applied in-process:
\begin{equation}
  \text{burnRate} = \text{clamp}_{[0,1]}\!\left(
    0.07 \cdot \text{mfs} + 0.03 \cdot d + 0.02 \cdot ra - 0.05
    + \text{noise}
  \right)
  \label{eq:mock-formula}
\end{equation}

\noindent where \textit{mfs} represents the mental fatigue score,
\textit{d} represents designation, \textit{ra} represents resource
allocation, and \textit{noise} is uniform random in
$[\!-0.025, +0.025]$. No code changes are needed to switch between
modes, only an environment variable update and a server restart.

\section{Discussion and Evaluation}

\subsection{Model Performance Analysis}

The Random Forest model scored an $R^{2}$ of 0.90, an MAE of 0.048, and
an RMSE of 0.061 on the held-out test set
(Table~\ref{tab:model-comparison}). These numbers mean the model explains
90\% of the variance in burn rate, and its predictions are usually
within 5 percentage points on the 0-1 scale. Compared to Linear
Regression, the Random Forest did 3.6 percentage points better in
$R^{2}$, which shows it can capture non-linear interactions between the
three features, something a linear model cannot do without extra feature
engineering.

Feature importance analysis reveals that the mental fatigue score makes
up about 85\% of the model's predictive power. Resource allocation
contributes around 10\%, and designation about 5\%. This lines up with
domain knowledge: an employee's psychological state is the clearest
indicator of burnout risk, while workload intensity and seniority matter
less. The strong role played by the mental fatigue score also supports
the system design, where the CBI quiz, the source of this score, is
placed at the center of the assessment workflow.

The residual analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:residual-analysis}) shows that
prediction errors are scattered randomly around zero with no obvious
pattern. This indicates the model does not consistently over-predict or
under-predict across the burn rate range. The residuals are roughly
normally distributed and centered at zero, which is a good sign for a
regression model.

When compared to public Kaggle notebooks trained on the same dataset
using all available features, the system's $R^{2}$ of 0.90, using only
three features, fits within the reported range of 0.90-0.93 for
Random Forest models. This suggests the three selected features capture
most of the useful signal, and the extra columns (Gender, Company Type,
WFH Setup) do not add much.

\subsection{Answering the Research Questions}

\textbf{RQ1: How effectively can a Random Forest regression model predict
employee burn rate using only three input features?}

An $R^{2}$ of 0.90 and an MAE of 0.048 are achieved by the Random
Forest model. This demonstrates that three features: designation,
resource allocation, and mental fatigue score are enough for effective
burn rate prediction. Published results using the full feature set are
comparable to the model's performance, which indicates that the dominant
signal in the data is captured by the three selected features.

\textbf{RQ2: Can a web-based system provide actionable burnout insights to
both employees and HR administrators?}

The employee portal provides personalised burn rate gauges, historical
trend charts, section score breakdowns, and contextual help resources,
enabling workers to monitor their own mental health proactively. The admin
portal provides department heatmaps, risk distribution charts,
KPI-vs-burn-rate correlations, and automated alerts, giving HR managers
actionable visibility into organisational burnout patterns. The functional
testing results confirm that all data flows correctly from quiz submission
through prediction to dashboard visualisation.

\textbf{RQ3: What are the practical benefits and trade-offs of using a
microservice architecture to separate ML inference from the application
backend?}

The microservice architecture provides three concrete benefits:
(1)~technology heterogeneity, Python's scikit-learn ecosystem for ML and
JavaScript's Express ecosystem for web logic are each used where they excel;
(2)~independent development, the AI service can be retrained and redeployed
without modifying or restarting the backend; (3)~the mock/real switch
(\texttt{AI\_USE\_MOCK}) enables fully functional development without running
the Python service. The primary trade-off is increased operational
complexity: two servers must be running and communicating, and network
failures between them require timeout handling and error propagation.

\subsection{Comparison with Related Work}

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:related-comparison}, this system is the only
one among the reviewed works that brings together all four elements: a
trained ML model, a deployed web application, organisational-level
analytics, and a validated burnout instrument (CBI).

Pradhan and Jena's framework~\cite{pradhan2019} applies machine learning
for burnout classification, but it does not include a web deployment or
organisational dashboards. Commercial platforms like Culture
Amp~\cite{cultureamp2024} provide web-based analytics, yet they depend on
general-purpose engagement surveys rather than ML prediction. Kaggle
community notebooks~\cite{kaggle2021} show model training on the same
dataset, but predictions are not integrated into a usable application.

This thesis addresses the integration gap by combining psychometric
assessment, machine learning prediction, and multi-level analytics into a
single platform that serves both individual workers and organisational
decision makers.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

\subsection{Conclusion}

This thesis presented the design, implementation, and evaluation of an
intelligent web-based framework for the early detection of employee burnout
in industrial workplaces. The system brings together a validated
psychometric instrument (the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory), a machine
learning prediction model (Random Forest regression), and a full-stack
web application with role-based portals for both employees and
administrators.

The Random Forest model, trained on approximately 22{,}000 records from a
public dataset, achieves an $R^{2}$ of 0.90 using only three input
features. This demonstrates that effective burn rate prediction is
possible even with a minimal feature set. The employee portal equips
workers with self-monitoring tools, including burn rate gauges, trend
charts, and contextual support resources. Meanwhile, the admin portal
offers organisational-level visibility through department heatmaps, risk
distribution analytics, KPI correlations, and automated alerts.

The microservice architecture successfully separates the machine learning
concern (Python/FastAPI) from the web application backend
(Node.js/Express), which allows independent development and deployment.
End-to-end functional testing confirmed that all core workflows operate
correctly, from quiz submission and scoring through AI prediction to
dashboard visualisation and alert generation.

\subsection{Contributions}

This thesis makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An integrated burnout detection platform} that combines
    psychometric assessment, machine learning prediction, and
    multi-level organisational analytics within a single web-based
    system. This combination is not found in any of the reviewed related
    works.

  \item \textbf{A practical CBI-to-ML pipeline} that transforms
    13-question Copenhagen Burnout Inventory responses into a continuous
    burn rate prediction through a clearly documented scoring and
    feature engineering process. This includes reverse scoring, section
    averaging, and normalisation.

  \item \textbf{A demonstrable microservice pattern} for integrating
    machine learning into web applications, with a clean mock/real
    switch mechanism that enables independent development of the ML and
    web components.

  \item \textbf{A comprehensive admin analytics suite} including
    department heatmaps, risk distribution charts, KPI-vs-burn-rate
    correlations, and automated three-type alert generation
    (high risk, critical risk, and trend spike).

  \item \textbf{A complete design system and component library} with
    15 reusable UI primitives and 12 data visualisation components,
    including a custom SVG burn rate gauge.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Achievement of Objectives}

All six objectives defined in Section~1.4 have been fully achieved:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The CBI-based assessment is implemented as a two-step wizard with
    proper reverse scoring and mental fatigue score derivation.

  \item The Random Forest model achieves $R^{2} = 0.90$, MAE = 0.048,
    and RMSE = 0.061, significantly outperforming the Linear Regression
    baseline.

  \item The employee portal includes four fully functional pages:
    dashboard, assessment, results, and history.

  \item The admin portal includes six pages: command centre, department
    detail (with employees and analytics tabs), company-wide employee

    management.

  \item Three alert types (high risk, critical risk, and trend spike) are
    automatically generated and displayed in a filterable inbox with
    mark-read functionality.

  \item The three-service architecture (React/Next.js, Node.js/Express,
    Python/FastAPI) is fully operational with mock/real mode switching.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations should be acknowledged:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulated HR data.} Designation and resource allocation
    values are randomly generated at registration rather than sourced
    from a real factory HR system. While values are drawn from the
    training dataset's ranges, they do not reflect actual workplace
    conditions.

  \item \textbf{Limited feature set.} The model uses only three
    features. Predictive accuracy could be improved by including
    additional signals such as actual shift patterns, overtime records,
    or longitudinal CBI trends.

  \item \textbf{Single dataset.} Model training and evaluation rely on a
    single public Kaggle dataset. The model's performance may not
    generalise across different industries, cultures, or organisational
    contexts.

  \item \textbf{Simulated KPI data.} The employee KPI records
    (attendance, productivity, quality) are seeded rather than collected
    from real workplace systems, which limits the validity of the
    KPI-vs-burn-rate correlation analysis.

  \item \textbf{Desktop-only interface.} The application is designed for
    minimum 992~px screen widths. Factory workers using personal mobile
    devices cannot access the system.

  \item \textbf{No formal usability study.} The system was tested
    functionally but not evaluated through a structured usability study
    with real end users.

  \item \textbf{No real-time updates.} The admin dashboard uses
    polling-based data fetching via TanStack Query rather than real-time
    push notifications via WebSocket or Server-Sent Events.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{subsec:future}

The following directions are proposed for future development:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mobile responsive design.} Extend the frontend with
    responsive breakpoints to support mobile and tablet access, enabling
    factory workers to complete assessments on personal devices.

  \item \textbf{Real-time alerts.} Implement WebSocket or Server-Sent
    Events to push new alerts to the admin dashboard instantly, without
    requiring page refresh or polling intervals.

  \item \textbf{Integration with real HR systems.} Replace the simulated
    HR profile generator with connectors to actual HR information
    systems (e.g., SAP SuccessFactors, BambooHR) to provide authentic
    designation and workload data.

  \item \textbf{Advanced ML models.} Explore Gradient Boosting
    (XGBoost, LightGBM) and time-series models that incorporate
    longitudinal CBI trends, enabling the system to predict burn rate
    trajectories rather than point estimates.

  \item \textbf{Expanded feature engineering.} Incorporate additional
    features such as actual overtime hours, shift rotation frequency,
    and seasonal patterns to improve model accuracy.

  \item \textbf{Export functionality.} Add CSV and PDF export for
    department analytics reports and assessment history, enabling
    offline review and reporting.

  \item \textbf{Accessibility improvements.} Add ARIA labels to custom
    components (gauge, heatmap) and conduct automated accessibility
    testing to ensure compliance with WCAG guidelines.

  \item \textbf{Multi-language support.} Internationalise the user
    interface to support Vietnamese and other languages relevant to the
    target industrial workforce.

  \item \textbf{Formal usability evaluation.} Conduct a structured
    usability study with factory workers and HR personnel to validate
    the system's effectiveness and identify user experience
    improvements.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Appendix C: Copenhagen Burnout Inventory Questionnaire}
\addcontentsline{toc}{subsection}{Appendix C: CBI Questionnaire}

The system uses 13 questions from the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory,
covering two sections. Response options for all questions are: Always
(100), Often (75), Sometimes (50), Seldom (25), Never/almost never (0).

\subsection*{C.1 Personal Burnout (Questions 1--6)}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item How often do you feel tired?
  \item How often are you physically exhausted?
  \item How often are you emotionally exhausted?
  \item How often do you think: ``I can't take it anymore''?
  \item How often do you feel worn out?
  \item How often do you feel weak and susceptible to illness?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{C.2 Work-Related Burnout (Questions 7--13)}

\begin{enumerate}
  \setcounter{enumi}{6}
  \item Do you feel worn out at the end of the working day?
  \item Are you exhausted in the morning at the thought of another day
    at work?
  \item Do you feel that every working hour is tiring for you?
  \item Do you have enough energy for family and friends during leisure
    time? \textit{(Reverse-scored: adjustedValue = 100 $-$ rawValue)}
  \item Is your work emotionally exhausting?
  \item Does your work frustrate you?
  \item Do you feel burnt out because of your work?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{C.3 Scoring}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Calculate the personal burnout average: mean of questions 1--6
    (range 0--100).
  \item Calculate the work-related burnout average: mean of questions
    7--13 (range 0--100), with question~10 reverse-scored.
  \item Calculate the mental fatigue score:
    $\text{mentalFatigueScore} = \frac{\text{personalAvg} +
    \text{workAvg}}{20}$ (range 0.0--10.0).
\end{enumerate}
